## Introduction

An arithmetic quotient is locally as smooth as the Lie group from which it is made, yet globally it can have long narrowing ends. This contrast is the source of both its power and its difficulty. On a compact manifold, bounded energy prevents oscillation and the Rellich principle prevents mass from disappearing. On a quotient with cusps, local regularity still prevents oscillation, but a sequence may move farther and farther into an end. Any honest global theory must therefore solve two different problems: derivatives must control local roughness, and vanishing constant terms must control escape to infinity.

This book develops that division of labor. Invariant vector fields provide Sobolev norms that do not depend on a coordinate atlas. Ellipticity turns weak information into derivatives. Completeness removes artificial boundary conditions at infinity. Cusp coordinates expose a transverse compact quotient and a distinguished average, the constant term. Once that average vanishes, a transverse Poincare inequality becomes stronger and stronger down the cusp. It yields uniform tail bounds, which combine with compactness on a truncation to recover global compactness on cuspidal subspaces.

The analytic chain is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{invariant derivatives}
\Longrightarrow \text{Sobolev control}
\Longrightarrow \text{elliptic regularity},\\
&\text{vanishing constant terms}
\Longrightarrow \text{cusp decay}
\Longrightarrow \text{compact embedding},\\
&\text{compact embedding}
\Longrightarrow \text{discrete typewise spectrum}
\Longrightarrow \text{smoothing kernels and traces}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The preceding spectral foundations supply closed operators, quadratic forms, functional calculus, compact-resolvent theory, and trace ideals. We use those results rather than rebuilding them. The geometric input from the preceding study of real groups supplies maximal compact subgroups, invariant differential operators, and compact-type decompositions. Everything peculiar to noncompact quotients—the local and global elliptic estimates, weak regularity, cusp tail bounds, truncation argument, and kernel estimates—is proved here under explicit hypotheses.

Our principal setting is a torsion-free lattice $\Gamma$ in a unimodular real Lie group $G$. The quotient $X=\Gamma\backslash G$ is then a smooth manifold without boundary. Arithmeticity matters because later reduction theory supplies finitely many cusp charts of the form assumed in Chapter 6; none of the local analysis depends on the arithmetic construction itself. If a lattice has torsion, the same arguments apply in finite quotient charts, but we avoid silently replacing a manifold by an orbifold. If a noncompact split center is to be removed, it is removed before $X$ is defined.

All functions are complex-valued unless a finite-rank Hermitian bundle is specified. Measures are positive Borel measures. Inner products are linear in the first variable. Multi-indices are ordered words, because invariant vector fields need not commute. Constants may change from line to line, but their dependence is always stated when it matters.

## 1. Invariant geometry and quotient measure

The first task is to choose geometry that survives passage to a quotient. Coordinates are indispensable in proofs, but a coordinate-dependent norm would obscure group symmetries and make every change of cusp chart hazardous. Invariant frames solve this problem, provided the measure and the side of the group action are chosen compatibly.

### 1.1 The analytic setting

Let $G$ be a second-countable real Lie group of dimension $d$, let $\mathfrak g$ be its Lie algebra, and let $\Gamma\subset G$ be discrete and torsion-free. Left multiplication by $\Gamma$ acts freely and properly discontinuously. Therefore

$$
X=\Gamma\backslash G
$$

is a second-countable smooth $d$-manifold without boundary, and the quotient map $q:G\to X$ is a covering and a local diffeomorphism.

We assume throughout that $G$ is unimodular and that $\Gamma$ is a lattice: for one, hence every, Haar normalization the quotient has finite volume. Unimodularity means that a left Haar measure $dg$ is also right invariant. It is automatic for connected reductive groups and is essential for the unweighted integration-by-parts formulas below.

Choose a basis $X_1,\ldots,X_d$ of $\mathfrak g$. The curve $g\exp(tX_j)$ defines a left-invariant vector field on $G$: left translation carries the curve at $g$ to the corresponding curve at any other point. It commutes with left translation by $\Gamma$, so it descends to $X$. We use the same symbol $X_j$ for the descended field.

There is a right action of $G$ on $X$,

$$
R_h(\Gamma g)=\Gamma gh.
$$

Its infinitesimal derivatives are precisely the $X_j$. This is the compatibility needed later: invariant differential operators describe the regularity of orbit maps for the right action.

### 1.2 Invariant frames and completeness

Give $\mathfrak g$ an inner product for which $X_1,\ldots,X_d$ is orthonormal, and transport it by left translation. The resulting left-invariant Riemannian metric on $G$ descends to $X$, because $\Gamma$ acts on the left by isometries.

**Proposition 1.1.** The left-invariant metric on $G$ and the quotient metric on $X$ are complete.

**Proof strategy.** A homogeneous metric cannot develop a finite-distance edge: translate every sufficiently small displacement back to one fixed compact ball.

Choose $r>0$ so small that the closed metric ball $\overline B(e,r)$ is contained in a relatively compact coordinate neighborhood of the identity. If $(g_n)$ is Cauchy, fix $n_0$ so late that $d(g_{n_0},g_n)<r$ for $n\geq n_0$. Left invariance gives

$$
g_{n_0}^{-1}g_n\in\overline B(e,r).
$$

The tail therefore lies in the compact set $g_{n_0}\overline B(e,r)$ and has a convergent subsequence. A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges, proving metric completeness directly. Finite-dimensional Riemannian completeness then also gives geodesic completeness.

The covering map $q$ is a local isometry. Let $(x_n)$ be Cauchy in $X$. Choose $n_0$ so that all later points lie in a normal ball $B(x_{n_0},r_0)$ evenly covered by $q$. Lift the tail to the sheet containing a chosen lift of $x_{n_0}$. These lifts are Cauchy in $G$, converge by completeness, and project to the limit of $(x_n)$. Thus $X$ is complete. $\square$

Completeness concerns the original quotient, not a truncation. A truncated quotient has a genuine boundary and needs a boundary condition. Confusing these two spaces leads to spurious conditions at the artificial cusp cut.

### 1.3 Measure on a lattice quotient

Fix a measurable fundamental domain $F\subset G$ whose distinct translates are disjoint up to null sets and cover $G$ up to a null set. For every nonnegative measurable $\Gamma$-invariant function $\widetilde f$ define

$$
\int_X f(x)\,dx=\int_F\widetilde f(g)\,dg.
\tag{1.1}
$$

This does not depend on $F$. Indeed, if $F'$ is another fundamental domain, decompose $F$ into the measurable pieces $F\cap\gamma F'$. Left invariance and $\Gamma$-invariance give

$$
\int_{F\cap\gamma F'}\widetilde f(g)\,dg
=\int_{\gamma^{-1}F\cap F'}\widetilde f(g)\,dg.
$$

The pieces on each side are disjoint up to null sets and cover $F$ and $F'$, respectively. Countable additivity proves equality. The same argument, first for nonnegative functions and then for integrable functions, gives the quotient integration formula.

Because $G$ is unimodular, $dx$ is invariant under the right action. For $h\in G$, $Fh$ is again a fundamental domain and right invariance of $dg$ yields

$$
\int_X f(xh)\,dx=\int_X f(x)\,dx.
\tag{1.2}
$$

Consequently the operators $R_hf(x)=f(xh)$ are unitary on $L^2(X)$. Differentiating (1.2) for compactly supported smooth $u,v$ gives

$$
\langle X_j u,v\rangle=-\langle u,X_jv\rangle.
\tag{1.3}
$$

There is no boundary term: the support is compact in the boundaryless manifold $X$. For noncompactly supported functions, (1.3) is justified only after an approximation or domain argument.

If $G$ were not unimodular, right translation would multiply $dg$ by the modular character and the adjoint of $X_j$ would acquire a zeroth-order divergence term. The theory still exists, but the positive invariant Laplacian would have a different formula. We exclude that distraction because arithmetic reductive quotients lie in the unimodular setting.

### 1.4 Fundamental examples and cautions

For $G=\mathbb R$ and $\Gamma=\mathbb Z$, $X$ is a circle. The invariant derivative is $d/dx$, the quotient measure is ordinary length, and every global compactness statement is the familiar periodic one.

For $G=\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)$ and a torsion-free finite-index subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$, quotienting on the right by a maximal compact subgroup produces a finite-area hyperbolic surface. The measure in upper-half-plane coordinates is $dx\,dy/y^2$. The surface is complete and has no boundary, although the region $y\leq Y$ has boundary $y=Y$.

Two cautions will recur. First, finite volume does not imply compactness. The interval $[1,\infty)$ with measure $y^{-2}dy$ has finite measure, yet normalized bumps can move to infinity. Second, small injectivity radius does not destroy local regularity. A function on $X$ lifts to a $\Gamma$-invariant function on $G$, where one may apply the same fixed coordinate estimate around every lift. This lifting device replaces any nonexistent uniform lower bound on quotient injectivity radius.

## 2. Invariant Sobolev spaces

Sobolev spaces measure derivatives in an integral sense. On a group quotient the natural derivatives are already present, and their noncommutativity is an advantage rather than a defect: ordered products remember the entire algebra of infinitesimal symmetries.

### 2.1 Derivatives generated by the group

For a word $\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r)$ with letters in $\{1,\ldots,d\}$, put

$$
X^\alpha=X_{\alpha_1}\cdots X_{\alpha_r},
\qquad |\alpha|=r,
$$

and let the empty word have length zero and operator $I$. Order matters: $X_iX_j-X_jX_i=[X_i,X_j]$, a first-order operator. Thus changing the order changes only lower-order terms after a fixed ordered normal form is chosen.

For $m\in\mathbb N$ and $u\in C_c^\infty(X)$ define

$$
\|u\|_{H^m_{\mathrm{inv}}(X)}^2
=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\|X^\alpha u\|_{L^2(X)}^2.
\tag{2.1}
$$

The completion is $H^m_{\mathrm{inv}}(X)$. We normally omit the subscript. Including the zeroth-order term is important on a finite-volume noncompact space: derivatives alone do not control constants or almost-constant functions far down a cusp.

### 2.2 Integer-order norms and independence of choices

The basis and the ordering convention do not change the space.

**Proposition 2.1.** Any two bases of $\mathfrak g$, and any two norms formed by summing all ordered products of length at most $m$, define equivalent norms on $C_c^\infty(X)$, with constants depending only on the two bases and $m$.

**Proof.** If $Y_j=\sum_i a_{ji}X_i$, expanding $Y_{j_1}\cdots Y_{j_r}$ gives a finite linear combination of $X$-words of length $r$. The coefficients are products of entries of the fixed matrix $(a_{ji})$; no coefficient depends on the point of $X$. Hence the $Y$-norm is at most a constant times the $X$-norm. Apply the same argument to the inverse change-of-basis matrix for the reverse inequality. Commuting letters to another prescribed order introduces brackets, and every bracket is a fixed linear combination of the $X_i$, hence only lower-order words. $\square$

The invariance is stronger than coordinate independence. For $h\in G$, differentiation of $u(xh)$ gives

$$
X(R_hu)=R_h\big((\operatorname{Ad}(h^{-1})X)u\big).
\tag{2.2}
$$

Therefore $R_h$ acts boundedly on $H^m(X)$; on a compact set of $h$ the operator norm is uniformly bounded. It need not be an isometry for $m>0$, because the chosen inner product on $\mathfrak g$ need not be invariant under the full adjoint action.

### 2.3 Weak derivatives, density, and duality

A function $u\in L^2(X)$ has weak derivative $X_ju=v\in L^2(X)$ if

$$
\int_Xu\,\overline{X_j\phi}\,dx
=-\int_Xv\,\overline\phi\,dx
\tag{2.3}
$$

for every $\phi\in C_c^\infty(X)$. Repeated weak derivatives are defined successively. Formula (1.3) shows that this agrees with classical differentiation for smooth functions.

**Theorem 2.2.** The completion definition (2.1) equals the space of $L^2$ functions whose weak derivatives $X^\alpha u$ belong to $L^2$ for every $|\alpha|\leq m$. Moreover, $C_c^\infty(X)$ is dense in that graph norm.

**Proof strategy.** Closedness of weak differentiation gives one inclusion. Cutoffs reduce to compact support, and local convolution smooths without losing graph convergence.

If $u_n$ is Cauchy in (2.1), then $u_n\to u$ and $X^\alpha u_n\to v_\alpha$ in $L^2$. Passing to the limit in (2.3) identifies $v_\alpha$ as the weak derivative of $u$. Thus the completion lies in the weak space.

Conversely, let $u$ have all required weak derivatives. Choose a countable locally finite atlas by precompact coordinate balls and a smooth partition of unity $(\psi_j)$ subordinate to it. Each $\psi_ju$ has compact support in one chart. Extend its coordinate representative by zero inside a slightly larger chart and convolve with a Euclidean mollifier. The product rule shows that all weak derivatives up to order $m$ converge to those of $\psi_ju$ as the mollifier radius tends to zero: derivatives falling on smooth coefficients converge uniformly, and those falling on $u$ converge by translation continuity in $L^2$.

It remains to pass from local pieces to one compactly supported approximation. Group the locally finite family into compact shells. For the first $N$ shells, choose the mollifier radii so that the sum of all graph-norm errors is less than $2^{-N}$. Choose the shell boundaries outside a compact set on which the $L^2$ norms of every $X^\alpha u$, $|\alpha|\leq m$, have total tail less than $2^{-N}$ after multiplication by the finitely many partition derivatives meeting that boundary. This is possible by absolute continuity of the integral; the multiplier constants are finite on each selected compact shell. The product rule and local finiteness then show that the compact partial sums converge to $u$ in the full graph norm. This is the local smoothing argument with a diagonal exhaustion, and it proves the reverse inclusion. $\square$

Completeness is not needed for this density statement on a manifold without boundary. It becomes decisive later: a complete Riemannian manifold admits smooth compactly supported cutoffs $\chi_R$ with $0\leq\chi_R\leq1$, $\chi_R\to1$, and

$$
\|\nabla\chi_R\|_\infty\longrightarrow0.
\tag{2.4}
$$

Start with a piecewise linear cutoff of the distance from a base point and smooth it while preserving its Lipschitz constant up to an arbitrarily small error. Only the first-derivative bound is asserted. On an incomplete interval, such cutoffs cannot erase the endpoints without recording a boundary condition.

Define $H^{-m}(X)$ as the continuous conjugate dual of $H^m(X)$ with the $L^2$ pairing. Then

$$
\|f\|_{H^{-m}}
=\sup_{0\ne v\in H^m}\frac{|\langle f,v\rangle|}{\|v\|_{H^m}}.
\tag{2.5}
$$

Every expression $\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(X^\alpha)^*f_\alpha$ with $f_\alpha\in L^2$ defines an element of $H^{-m}$. Conversely, embed $H^m$ isometrically into the finite Hilbert sum of its derivatives. Extending a functional from the image and applying the Hilbert representation theorem gives such an expression. Thus negative Sobolev data are precisely finite sums of weak derivatives of $L^2$ functions.

### 2.4 Local comparison and Sobolev embedding

Let $U\Subset X$ lie in one smooth chart $\kappa:U\to V\subset\mathbb R^d$. On a smaller $U'\Subset U$, each $X_j$ is a smooth linear combination of coordinate derivatives, and the coefficient matrix is invertible. Iterating gives

$$
C^{-1}\|u\circ\kappa^{-1}\|_{H^m(\kappa(U'))}
\leq \|u\|_{H^m(U')}
\leq C\|u\circ\kappa^{-1}\|_{H^m(\kappa(U))}
\tag{2.6}
$$

for functions supported in $U'$. The right inequality follows by expanding invariant derivatives. For the left, solve coordinate derivatives in terms of the $X_j$ and induct on order; derivatives of the coefficient matrix contribute only lower-order terms.

The Euclidean Fourier estimate

$$
\|v\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb R^d)}
\leq C_s\|v\|_{H^s(\mathbb R^d)},
\qquad s>d/2,
\tag{2.7}
$$

follows from Fourier inversion and Cauchy--Schwarz because $(1+|\xi|^2)^{-s/2}$ is square-integrable exactly when $s>d/2$. Applying it to derivatives proves $H^s_{\mathrm{loc}}\subset C^r$ when $s>r+d/2$. On a compact subset, finitely many charts give one constant. On the whole noncompact quotient, a uniform pointwise constant requires the invariant lifting argument or a bounded-geometry hypothesis; it is not a formal consequence of local embedding.

### 2.5 Bundles, compact types, and central directions

Let $E\to X$ be a finite-rank Hermitian bundle associated with a unitary representation of a compact subgroup. In a unitary local frame, replace $X_j$ by a compatible covariant derivative $\nabla_{X_j}$. Two compatible invariant connections differ by a smooth endomorphism-valued one-form with invariantly bounded coefficients, so their integer Sobolev norms are equivalent. All local estimates below extend to such bundles, with scalar products replaced by fiber inner products.

If a compact group $K$ acts on the right, its isotypic projection is bounded on every $H^m$ because it is an average of right translations over a compact set and (2.2) is uniformly bounded there. On a fixed irreducible $K$-type, derivatives tangent to $K$ act through a finite-dimensional representation and are bounded by a constant depending on that type. This is why typewise ellipticity can be obtained by adding a positive compact-direction operator.

A noncompact central direction behaves differently. If it has not been quotiented out or fixed by a unitary character, translation along it supplies another escape channel. Any global compactness theorem must either include its derivative in an elliptic energy and impose confinement, or remove it before forming the finite-volume quotient.

## 3. Elliptic operators and a priori estimates

Sobolev norms list all derivatives; an elliptic operator packages them into one expression. The main estimate says that applying an elliptic operator of order $r$ controls all derivatives of order $r$, modulo a lower-order norm. That apparently small remainder is indispensable: kernels and low frequencies cannot be controlled by the principal symbol.

### 3.1 Symbols and the invariant Laplacian

Let

$$
P=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq r}a_\alpha(x)X^\alpha
$$

be a differential operator of order $r$ on a bundle $E$. Its principal symbol at $(x,\xi)\in T^*X$ is obtained by retaining $|\alpha|=r$ and replacing each $X_j$ by $i\xi(X_j(x))$. The operator is elliptic if this fiber map is invertible for every $\xi\ne0$. It is uniformly strongly elliptic of order two when its principal quadratic form has real part at least $c|\xi|^2$ for one $c>0$ in the invariant metric.

The fundamental positive operator is

$$
\Lambda=I-\sum_{j=1}^dX_j^2.
\tag{3.1}
$$

By (1.3),

$$
\langle\Lambda u,u\rangle
=\|u\|_2^2+\sum_j\|X_ju\|_2^2.
\tag{3.2}
$$

Its principal symbol is $\sum_j\xi(X_j)^2=|\xi|^2$, so it is elliptic and positive. The order of products in (3.1) causes no ambiguity because only squares occur. On a bundle one replaces $X_j$ by covariant derivatives and may add a bounded self-adjoint endomorphism to make the operator positive.

A central invariant operator alone need not be elliptic on the whole group: its symbol may miss compact tangent directions. Adding the compact-direction Laplacian repairs the symbol. After fixing a compact type that added term is merely a finite-dimensional scalar or matrix, so the spectral labels of interest are unchanged up to a controlled shift.

### 3.2 The interior estimate

**Theorem 3.1 (interior elliptic estimate).** Let $P$ be an elliptic operator of order $r$ with smooth coefficients from $E$ to $F$ on a smooth manifold. If $U'\Subset U$ are coordinate neighborhoods and $s\geq0$ is an integer, then

$$
\|u\|_{H^{s+r}(U')}
\leq C\bigl(\|Pu\|_{H^s(U)}+\|u\|_{L^2(U)}\bigr)
\tag{3.3}
$$

for every $u\in C_c^\infty(U;E)$. The constant depends on finitely many coefficient bounds, the ellipticity lower bound, the two coordinate sets, and $s$.

**Proof strategy.** Freeze the top-order coefficients, invert the constant symbol by Fourier transformation, absorb the variation on a small ball, and commute derivatives through the equation.

For a constant-coefficient elliptic operator $P_0$ of order $r$, compactness of the unit sphere and homogeneity give

$$
|\xi|^r|\widehat u(\xi)|
\leq C\bigl(|p_0(\xi)\widehat u(\xi)|+|\widehat u(\xi)|\bigr).
$$

After multiplication by $(1+|\xi|^2)^{s/2}$ and integration, Plancherel gives (3.3) on $\mathbb R^d$ with $U'=U$.

For variable coefficients, choose a ball so small that the top-order coefficients differ from their values at its center by at most $\varepsilon$. Write $P=P_0+Q$. The $Q$ term is bounded by $C\varepsilon\|u\|_{H^{s+r}}+C_\varepsilon\|u\|_{H^{s+r-1}}$. Fourier interpolation gives, for every $\delta>0$,

$$
\|u\|_{H^{s+r-1}}
\leq\delta\|u\|_{H^{s+r}}+C_\delta\|u\|_2.
$$

Choose first the ball and then $\delta$ so that both high-order terms are absorbed on the left. A cutoff equal to one on $U'$ creates only order $r-1$ commutator terms, handled by the same interpolation. Cover $\overline{U'}$ by finitely many such balls.

For $s>0$, apply the $s=0$ estimate to coordinate derivatives of order at most $s$. The identity

$$
P(D^\beta u)=D^\beta(Pu)+[P,D^\beta]u
$$

has a commutator of order at most $r+s-1$ with controlled coefficients. Induction on $s$ and interpolation finish the estimate. Bundle-valued operators are identical after choosing finite local frames. $\square$

The estimate is interior: no condition is imposed on the boundary of $U$ because the cutoff keeps the measured region $U'$ away from it.

### 3.3 A global invariant estimate

For invariant operators on $X=\Gamma\backslash G$, the local constants can be made uniform without assuming a positive injectivity radius.

**Theorem 3.2.** Let $P$ be a left-invariant elliptic operator of order $r$ on $G$, descending to $X$. For every integer $s\geq0$ there is $C_s$ such that

$$
\|u\|_{H^{s+r}(X)}
\leq C_s\bigl(\|Pu\|_{H^s(X)}+\|u\|_{L^2(X)}\bigr)
\tag{3.4}
$$

for all $u\in C_c^\infty(X)$.

**Proof.** Choose relatively compact identity neighborhoods $V'\Subset V\subset G$ on which Theorem 3.1 holds, and a cutoff $\eta$ supported in $V$ and equal to one on $V'$. Lift $u$ to a $\Gamma$-invariant function $\widetilde u$. For every $g\in G$, apply the translated local estimate to $h\mapsto\eta(h)\widetilde u(gh)$. Left invariance makes all coefficient and cutoff constants independent of $g$.

Square the estimate and integrate $g$ over a fundamental domain. Every term has the form

$$
\int_F\int_V |D\widetilde u(gh)|^2\,dh\,dg.
$$

Right invariance and the quotient formula turn this into $\operatorname{vol}(V)\|Du\|_2^2$. The same identity holds with $V'$ on the left; choose finitely many right translates of $V'$ covering a neighborhood used by the norm, or equivalently sum over a finite translated partition of unity. Commutators with $\eta$ have lower order and are absorbed by interpolation exactly as in Theorem 3.1. This yields (3.4). Notice that no quotient chart was required to be injective on a ball of fixed radius; periodic lifting did all the work. $\square$

For operators whose coefficients are merely uniformly bounded with uniformly bounded derivatives and a uniform ellipticity constant in the invariant frame, the same proof works. In cusp coordinates, this is the useful notion of uniformity.

### 3.4 Boundary conditions on truncations

Let $M$ be a compact smooth Riemannian manifold with smooth boundary, with density given by a smooth positive multiple of Riemannian volume. For a strongly elliptic symmetric second-order expression $P$, the Green formula has the form

$$
\langle Pu,v\rangle-\langle u,Pv\rangle
=\int_{\partial M}\bigl(Bu\,\overline v-u\,\overline{Bv}\bigr)\,d\sigma,
\tag{3.5}
$$

where $B$ is the conormal first derivative and $d\sigma$ is the induced boundary density.

Dirichlet conditions use the form domain $H_0^1(M)$, the closure of compactly supported smooth functions in the interior. Neumann conditions use $H^1(M)$ and arise naturally from the energy form; the operator domain then satisfies $Bu=0$ in the trace sense. Robin conditions $Bu+bu=0$ are allowed for a real bounded boundary coefficient $b$ after adding $\int_{\partial M}b|u|^2$ to the form.

The boundary estimate is

$$
\|u\|_{H^{s+2}(M)}
\leq C_s\bigl(\|Pu\|_{H^s(M)}+\|u\|_2\bigr),
\tag{3.6}
$$

provided $u$ satisfies one of these elliptic boundary conditions and, for $s>0$, the usual compatibility conditions. To prove it, flatten the boundary to a half-ball. Tangential difference quotients are controlled as in the interior. The equation solves for the second normal derivative because strong ellipticity makes its coefficient invertible. The boundary condition controls the first normal trace. Reflection proves the Dirichlet model estimate; an even reflection proves the flat Neumann model. Variable coefficients and curved boundary are absorbed after localization. This also explains why an arbitrary boundary condition is not acceptable: it must supply precisely the missing normal information.

### 3.5 Completeness and the canonical realization

On the complete boundaryless manifold $X$, begin with the positive symmetric operator $\Lambda$ on $C_c^\infty(X)$. Its quadratic form is

$$
\mathfrak q[u]=\|u\|_2^2+\sum_j\|X_ju\|_2^2,
\qquad D(\overline{\mathfrak q})=H^1(X).
\tag{3.7}
$$

The form is closed by Theorem 2.2 and defines a positive self-adjoint operator $A$.

**Theorem 3.3.** $\Lambda$ is essentially self-adjoint on $C_c^\infty(X)$, and its closure is $A$. Moreover,

$$
D(A)=H^2(X),
\qquad
\|u\|_{H^2}\asymp\|Au\|_2+\|u\|_2.
\tag{3.8}
$$

**Proof strategy.** Show that no nonzero $L^2$ solution of $(\Lambda^*+1)u=0$ can hide at infinity, using complete cutoffs; then use the global elliptic estimate to identify the domain.

Elliptic regularity from Chapter 4, initially used locally, makes any deficiency vector smooth. Let $\chi_R$ be the cutoff from (2.4). Testing $(\Lambda^*+1)u=0$ against $\chi_R^2u$ and integrating by parts gives

$$
2\|\chi_Ru\|_2^2+\sum_j\|\chi_RX_ju\|_2^2
\leq C\sum_j\|(X_j\chi_R)u\|_2^2.
$$

The right side tends to zero because $\|X_j\chi_R\|_\infty\to0$ and $u\in L^2$. Hence $u=0$. The same argument for the other nonreal deficiency equation, or the range criterion after a harmless shift, proves essential self-adjointness.

If $u\in D(A)$, choose $u_n\in C_c^\infty$ converging in the graph norm. Estimate (3.4) makes $(u_n)$ Cauchy in $H^2$, so $u\in H^2$. Conversely, if $u\in H^2$, then $\Lambda u\in L^2$ weakly, and the form identity shows $u\in D(A)$. Estimate (3.4) gives norm equivalence. $\square$

Completeness cannot be deleted. On $(0,1)$, the expression $-d^2/dx^2$ on compactly supported smooth functions has distinct Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin realizations. The missing endpoints behave exactly like unrecorded boundaries.

## 4. Weak solutions and elliptic regularity

The value of ellipticity is most visible when a solution is not initially smooth. Variational methods naturally produce only one weak derivative. Elliptic regularity then shows that the equation contains the missing derivatives implicitly.

### 4.1 Distributional and variational solutions

Let $P$ be an order-$r$ operator with formal adjoint $P^*$. A distribution $u$ solves $Pu=f$ on an open set $\Omega$ when

$$
\langle u,P^*\phi\rangle=\langle f,\phi\rangle
\tag{4.1}
$$

for every compactly supported smooth test section $\phi$. No boundary condition is encoded in this definition.

For a strongly elliptic second-order operator in divergence form,

$$
Pu=-\nabla_i(a^{ij}\nabla_ju)+b^j\nabla_ju+cu,
$$

a variational solution lies in $H^1$ and satisfies

$$
\mathfrak a[u,v]=\langle f,v\rangle
\tag{4.2}
$$

for all $v$ in the chosen form domain. Dirichlet or Neumann information enters through that domain. If $\operatorname{Re}a^{ij}\xi_i\overline{\xi_j}\geq c_0|\xi|^2$ and the lower-order terms are bounded, then after adding a sufficiently large multiple of $\|u\|_2^2$,

$$
\operatorname{Re}\mathfrak a[u,u]\geq c\|u\|_{H^1}^2.
\tag{4.3}
$$

The Hilbert representation theorem therefore gives a unique weak solution to $(P+\lambda)u=f$ for $\lambda$ large, under Dirichlet conditions or after removing the finite-dimensional Neumann kernel.

### 4.2 Difference quotients and the gain of two derivatives

**Theorem 4.1.** Let $P$ be uniformly strongly elliptic of order two with smooth coefficients. If $u\in H^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$ satisfies $Pu=f$ weakly and $f\in H^s_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$, then

$$
u\in H^{s+2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
$$

for every integer $s\geq0$. On $U'\Subset U\Subset\Omega$,

$$
\|u\|_{H^{s+2}(U')}
\leq C\bigl(\|f\|_{H^s(U)}+\|u\|_{L^2(U)}\bigr).
\tag{4.4}
$$

**Proof strategy.** The a priori estimate was proved for smooth functions. Difference quotients justify applying the same energy reasoning before the second derivatives are known.

Work first in a coordinate ball and take a cutoff $\eta$ supported there. For a coordinate direction $e_k$, define

$$
\delta_hu(x)=\frac{u(x+he_k)-u(x)}h.
$$

Use $-\delta_{-h}(\eta^2\delta_hu)$ as a test function in (4.2). Discrete integration by parts moves $\delta_h$ from the test function to the coefficients and $u$. Strong ellipticity supplies

$$
c_0\|\eta\nabla\delta_hu\|_2^2
\leq C\bigl(\|f\|_2^2+\|u\|_{H^1}^2\bigr)
+\frac{c_0}{2}\|\eta\nabla\delta_hu\|_2^2,
$$

where coefficient difference quotients are uniformly bounded and cutoff terms are handled by Cauchy--Schwarz. Absorb the last term. The bound is independent of small $h$. Weak compactness and the characterization of weak derivatives by bounded difference quotients give all second derivatives of $u$ in $L^2$ on the smaller ball.

The equation and the first-order energy estimate control the displayed right side by $\|f\|_2+\|u\|_2$, yielding (4.4) for $s=0$. For higher $s$, differentiate the equation weakly. Commutators have one fewer derivative on $u$ than the leading term, so induction applies. Localization and the norm comparison (2.6) return the manifold statement. $\square$

For an elliptic operator of order $r$, the same conclusion is a gain of $r$ derivatives, proved by the Fourier localization argument of Theorem 3.1 and approximation. The second-order difference-quotient proof is singled out because it makes the weak mechanism transparent.

### 4.3 Bootstrapping and smoothness

If $f\in C^\infty(\Omega)$ and $Pu=f$ distributionally, first assume only $u$ is a distribution. On every compact coordinate ball, a distribution has finite order and therefore belongs to $H^{-N}$ for some $N$: its action is bounded by finitely many sup norms of test-function derivatives, and Fourier Cauchy--Schwarz bounds those sup norms by $H^N$ once $N$ is large enough. Dualizing the localized estimate (3.3), or applying it to mollifications and passing to weak limits, gives

$$
Pu\in H^s_{\mathrm{loc}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u\in H^{s+2}_{\mathrm{loc}}
$$

for negative integers as well. Starting at $s=-N$ reaches $L^2$ after finitely many steps; Theorem 4.1 then raises regularity two orders at a time. For every $m$, $u\in H^m_{\mathrm{loc}}$. Sobolev embedding yields $u\in C^r$ for every $r$, hence $u$ is smooth.

More quantitatively, if $Pu=\lambda u$ with $u\in L^2(X)$, local regularity makes $u$ smooth and the global estimate gives recursively

$$
\|u\|_{H^{2m}(X)}
\leq C_m(1+|\lambda|)^m\|u\|_2.
\tag{4.5}
$$

Thus eigenfunctions are not merely smooth; every invariant derivative is bounded in $L^2$ by a polynomial in the eigenvalue. This estimate later converts spectral decay into kernel convergence.

### 4.4 Lower-order terms and systems

Let $P=P_0+Q$, where $P_0$ is uniformly elliptic of order $r$ and $Q$ has order at most $r-1$ with uniformly bounded coefficients and derivatives. Interpolation gives

$$
\|Qu\|_{H^s}
\leq\varepsilon\|u\|_{H^{s+r}}+C_\varepsilon\|u\|_{L^2}.
$$

Insert this into the estimate for $P_0$ and absorb the first term. Hence all preceding estimates survive lower-order perturbations. Symmetry is irrelevant for local regularity; it matters only for the spectral realization.

For a system on a finite-rank bundle, ellipticity means invertibility of the matrix principal symbol. Strong ellipticity is the coercive inequality on fiber-valued covectors. The frozen-symbol proof and difference-quotient proof use finite-dimensional matrix norms and go through unchanged. Constants depend on rank, the inverse-symbol bound, and finitely many coefficient derivatives.

### 4.5 What fails without ellipticity

The operator $\partial_x$ on $\mathbb R^2$ controls no derivative in the $y$ direction. Functions $u_n(x,y)=\phi(x)e^{iny}\psi(y)$ have $\|\partial_xu_n\|_2$ bounded while their $y$ derivatives grow like $n$. Thus an estimate resembling (3.3) cannot hold.

Even a sum of squares may fail to be elliptic. On $\mathbb R^2$, $-\partial_x^2$ is positive and symmetric but its principal symbol vanishes when $\xi_x=0$. Positivity is not a substitute for ellipticity. In representation-theoretic settings, a central operator can have exactly this defect along compact directions, explaining the compact-direction correction introduced in Section 3.1.

## 5. Smooth vectors and Sobolev scales

Invariant derivatives were chosen because they differentiate the group action. We now prove the converse: membership in every invariant Sobolev space is exactly smoothness of the orbit map. This converts an analytic condition into an intrinsic representation-theoretic one.

### 5.1 Differentiability of orbit maps

Let $R$ be the right regular unitary representation on $L^2(X)$. If $u\in H^1(X)$, then for $Y\in\mathfrak g$,

$$
\frac{R_{\exp(tY)}u-u}{t}\longrightarrow Yu
\quad\text{in }L^2
\tag{5.1}
$$

as $t\to0$. For smooth compactly supported $u$, this is the fundamental theorem of calculus:

$$
R_{\exp(tY)}u-u
=\int_0^tR_{\exp(sY)}Yu\,ds.
$$

Divide by $t$, use strong continuity, and then pass to general $H^1$ functions by density and the unitarity of $R$.

Iterating (5.1) shows that if all words of length at most $m$ lie in $L^2$, then the orbit map $g\mapsto R_gu$ is $m$ times differentiable as an $L^2$-valued map, with derivatives given by the corresponding ordered words after the adjoint change dictated by (2.2).

### 5.2 The smooth-vector criterion

**Theorem 5.1.** For $u\in L^2(X)$, the following are equivalent.

1. The orbit map $g\mapsto R_gu$ is smooth from $G$ to $L^2(X)$.
2. Every ordered invariant derivative $X^\alpha u$ exists weakly and belongs to $L^2(X)$.
3. $u\in\bigcap_{m\geq0}H^m(X)$.
4. $u$ lies in the domain of every power of the positive self-adjoint realization $A$ of $\Lambda$.

**Proof.** The equivalence of (2) and (3) is Theorem 2.2. Section 5.1 proves (3) implies (1). If the orbit map is smooth, differentiate it at the identity and pair with a test function; this produces every weak derivative and proves (1) implies (2).

If $u\in D(A^k)$, repeated use of (3.8) gives $u\in H^{2k}$. Conversely, $u\in H^{2k}$ implies $\Lambda^ju\in H^{2k-2j}\subset L^2$ for $j\leq k$, so the domain characterization gives $u\in D(A^k)$. Taking all $k$ proves equivalence with (4). $\square$

This result does not say that every smooth function on $X$ is a smooth $L^2$ vector. A smooth function whose derivatives are not square-integrable is excluded. The correct phrase is global $L^2$ smoothness.

### 5.3 Spectral Sobolev norms

For real $s\geq0$, define

$$
H_A^s=D(A^{s/2}),
\qquad
\|u\|_{H_A^s}=\|A^{s/2}u\|_2.
\tag{5.2}
$$

Since $A\geq I$, this is a norm. For even integers, (3.8) and induction show equivalence with the invariant norm. Interpolation between adjacent integers extends equivalence to every real $s\geq0$. Negative orders are obtained by duality. The benefit is multiplicativity:

$$
A^{t/2}:H_A^s\longrightarrow H_A^{s-t}
$$

is an isometry whenever the domain is interpreted spectrally.

On a fixed compact type, one may replace $A$ by a positive elliptic operator adapted to the central infinitesimal character. Elliptic estimates show that the resulting scale is the same, with constants depending on the type. Thus smoothness is independent of the particular positive elliptic measuring operator.

### 5.4 Convolution as a smoothing operation

For $f\in C_c^\infty(G)$ define

$$
R(f)u(x)=\int_G f(g)u(xg)\,dg.
\tag{5.3}
$$

Differentiating under the integral and changing variables give

$$
X^\alpha R(f)u=R(L^\alpha f)u,
\tag{5.4}
$$

where $L^\alpha$ is the corresponding product of derivatives on the test function, with signs and adjoint conjugations fixed by the change of variables. Young's inequality yields

$$
\|R(f)u\|_{H^m}
\leq C_m\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\|L^\alpha f\|_{L^1(G)}\|u\|_2.
\tag{5.5}
$$

To justify differentiation, first take $u$ smooth, use compact support of $f$ for domination, and then pass to arbitrary $u\in L^2$ by density and (5.5). Hence convolution maps $L^2$ continuously into every $H^m$, and therefore into the smooth-vector space.

Smoothing is not compactness. On $L^2(\mathbb R)$, convolution commutes with translations, so a translated orthonormal sequence remains translated after smoothing and has no convergent subsequence. Compactness on a quotient requires the confinement mechanism developed next.

## 6. Cusp coordinates and constant terms

A cusp is not merely a noncompact end. Its cross-sections contract in the quotient metric while the invariant derivatives tangent to them expand. A function with zero average on each cross-section must oscillate there, and the expanding derivatives make that oscillation increasingly expensive. This is the analytic source of cuspidal decay.

### 6.1 The standard cusp hypothesis

We isolate exactly the geometry used in this and the next three chapters. Assume that a compact set $X_0\subset X$ has smooth boundary and that

$$
X\setminus X_0=\bigsqcup_{c=1}^h\mathcal C_c
$$

is a finite disjoint union of ends. For each $c$ there is a diffeomorphism

$$
\mathcal C_c\simeq[T_0,\infty)\times Y_c,
\tag{6.1}
$$

where $Y_c$ is a compact connected smooth manifold without boundary, supplied with a smooth positive density $dy$. In these coordinates the quotient measure is

$$
dx=e^{-2\rho_ct}a_c(t,y)\,dt\,dy,
\tag{6.2}
$$

where $\rho_c>0$, $a_c$ is smooth, and for every invariant derivative $D$ there are constants

$$
0<a_-\leq a_c\leq a_+,
\qquad |Da_c|\leq C_D.
\tag{6.3}
$$

There are vector fields $V_{c,1},\ldots,V_{c,q_c}$ spanning $TY_c$ and positive numbers $\lambda_{c,j}$ such that the invariant frame on the end is uniformly equivalent, with all derivatives, to

$$
\partial_t,qquad W_{c,j}=e^{\lambda_{c,j}t}V_{c,j},
\tag{6.4}
$$

together with any compact-fiber directions already included among the $V_{c,j}$. Put $\lambda_c=\min_j\lambda_{c,j}>0$. More general parabolic ends have several height variables; every argument below applies in each outward cone after replacing $\lambda_ct$ by the least positive root on that cone.

The hypotheses include all facts about the end used here: finite multiplicity of cusps, compact cross-sections, the exact density, uniform coefficient bounds, and exponential expansion of transverse invariant derivatives. Establishing these coordinates for particular arithmetic groups is reduction theory and belongs to the next stage of the subject.

Define the constant term on the $c$-th end by normalized fiber average,

$$
(\mathcal C_cu)(t)
=\frac1{\operatorname{vol}(Y_c)}\int_{Y_c}u(t,y)\,dy.
\tag{6.5}
$$

For bundles or a fixed compact type, this is the orthogonal projection onto the finite-dimensional transverse zero modes. Requiring all proper parabolic constant terms to vanish reduces in each cusp chart to the assertion that this projection is zero. We call a smooth function cuspidal on the end if $\mathcal C_cu=0$ for every $t\geq T_0$.

### 6.2 Constant terms and the transverse Poincare inequality

**Lemma 6.1 (fiber Poincare inequality).** Let $Y$ be a compact connected Riemannian manifold without boundary. There is $C_Y$ such that

$$
\|v-v_Y\|_{L^2(Y)}
\leq C_Y\|\nabla_Yv\|_{L^2(Y)},
\qquad
v_Y=\frac1{\operatorname{vol}(Y)}\int_Yv.
\tag{6.6}
$$

**Proof.** The variance identity is

$$
\|v-v_Y\|_2^2
=\frac1{2\operatorname{vol}(Y)}
\int_Y\int_Y|v(y)-v(z)|^2\,dy\,dz.
$$

Cover $Y$ by finitely many geodesically convex coordinate balls and join any two points by a chain of at most $N$ such balls, with $N$ independent of the points. Along a minimizing segment in one ball,

$$
|v(y)-v(z)|^2
\leq \operatorname{length}(\gamma)
\int_\gamma|\nabla v|^2.
$$

Integrating over the endpoints in the ball and using bounded coordinate Jacobians bounds the double integral by a constant times $\|\nabla v\|_2^2$. Summing over the finite chain proves (6.6) first for smooth $v$, and density proves it for $H^1(Y)$. Connectedness is necessary: a function constant with different values on two components has zero gradient and nonzero mean-zero part. $\square$

Because the $V_{c,j}$ span $TY_c$, their squared norms are uniformly equivalent to $\|\nabla_Yv\|_2^2$. If $\mathcal C_cu(t)=0$, (6.4) and (6.6) give the strengthened slice estimate

$$
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^2(Y_c)}^2
\leq Ce^{-2\lambda_ct}
\sum_j\|W_{c,j}u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^2(Y_c)}^2.
\tag{6.7}
$$

The exponential factor is the entire point. Without the zero average, a transverse constant violates (6.7).

### 6.3 Quantitative tail decay

Multiply (6.7) by the density in (6.2) and integrate for $t\geq T$. The bounded factors $a_c$ give

$$
\|u\|_{L^2(\mathcal C_c(T))}
\leq Ce^{-\lambda_cT}\|u\|_{H^1(\mathcal C_c(T))},
\tag{6.8}
$$

where $\mathcal C_c(T)=[T,\infty)\times Y_c$. The constant is independent of $T\geq T_0$.

Every tangential invariant derivative of a cuspidal function is again cuspidal: the integral of a derivative on compact $Y_c$ vanishes. The $t$ derivative also preserves zero average because differentiation passes through (6.5). Apply (6.7) successively to derivatives. For every integer $N\geq1$,

$$
\|u\|_{L^2(\mathcal C_c(T))}
\leq C_Ne^{-N\lambda_cT}\|u\|_{H^N(\mathcal C_c(T))}.
\tag{6.9}
$$

At each iteration, commutators among invariant fields produce lower-order terms. They are already present in the $H^N$ norm, and their structure constants do not depend on $t$. Formula (6.9) is rapid decay in an energy sense: arbitrary exponential gain is purchased with finitely many derivatives.

For a function that is only asymptotically cuspidal, write $u=(u-\mathcal C_cu)+\mathcal C_cu$. The first term obeys (6.8), while the second is controlled separately by a one-dimensional weighted norm. This separation is the basic distinction between cuspidal and continuous spectral directions.

### 6.4 Rapid decay of smooth cuspidal vectors

Let $u$ belong to every invariant Sobolev space and have zero constant term. Fix a differential word $D$ and an integer $M$. We claim

$$
\sup_{y\in Y_c}|Du(t,y)|\leq C_{D,M}e^{-Mt}
\quad(t\geq T_0+1).
\tag{6.10}
$$

Choose an integer $m>(\dim Y_c+1)/2$. Sobolev embedding on the fixed cylinder $[t-1,t+1]\times Y_c$ bounds the value at height $t$ by finitely many unscaled derivatives on that cylinder. Replacing $V_{c,j}$ by $e^{-\lambda_{c,j}s}W_{c,j}$ only improves the estimate. Converting unweighted cylinder measure to (6.2) costs at most $Ce^{\rho_ct}$. Apply (6.9), with $N$ so large that $N\lambda_c> M+\rho_c$, to each derivative. This proves (6.10).

Thus a globally smooth cuspidal vector is rapidly decreasing with all invariant derivatives. Smoothness alone is insufficient: the constant function is smooth and square-integrable on a finite-volume quotient, but it does not decay pointwise down a cusp.

### 6.5 The hyperbolic cusp as a model

On the standard surface cusp

$$
[T_0,\infty)\times(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z),
\qquad ds^2=dt^2+e^{-2t}dx^2,
$$

the volume is $e^{-t}dt\,dx$, so $2\rho=1$. The invariant transverse derivative is $W=e^t\partial_x$, hence $\lambda=1$. If $\int_0^1u(t,x)dx=0$, ordinary Fourier series gives

$$
\int_0^1|u(t,x)|^2dx
\leq\frac1{4\pi^2}\int_0^1|\partial_xu(t,x)|^2dx
=\frac{e^{-2t}}{4\pi^2}\int_0^1|Wu(t,x)|^2dx.
$$

This is (6.7) with an exact constant. The zero Fourier mode feels only the half-line variable $t$; all nonzero modes acquire a potential of size $4\pi^2n^2e^{2t}$. The contrast between a free channel and an exponentially confining channel is the spectral content of the constant-term decomposition.

## 7. Truncation and compactness

Local compactness is recovered by cutting the cusps at finite height. Global compactness then follows if all bounded-energy vectors have uniformly small tails. This two-step argument is more informative than an abstract compactness assertion because it identifies exactly what fails on the full space.

### 7.1 Smooth truncation and its boundary

For $T\geq T_0$, define

$$
X_T=X_0\cup\bigcup_c[T_0,T]\times Y_c.
\tag{7.1}
$$

After smoothing the finitely many corners where the original compact core meets the cusp collars, $X_T$ is a compact smooth manifold with boundary

$$
\partial X_T=\bigsqcup_c\{T\}\times Y_c.
$$

The boundary is artificial: it is not part of $X$. Its outward unit normal is uniformly comparable to $\partial_t$. Restriction $H^1(X)\to H^1(X_T)$ is bounded. No boundary condition is imposed when restriction is used merely for compactness.

When an operator is defined intrinsically on $X_T$, Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin conditions must be declared as in Section 3.4. Estimates claimed uniformly in $T$ must be checked in translated cusp collars. The invariant form (6.4) and bounds (6.3) make these local constants independent of the height after the natural rescaling.

### 7.2 Rellich compactness on a compact manifold with boundary

**Theorem 7.1.** If $M$ is a compact smooth manifold, possibly with smooth boundary, and $E\to M$ is a finite-rank bundle, the inclusion

$$
H^1(M;E)\hookrightarrow L^2(M;E)
\tag{7.2}
$$

is compact. No boundary condition is required.

**Proof strategy.** Reduce to bounded Euclidean cubes, where translation control makes bounded sets precompact.

In a cube $Q\subset\mathbb R^d$, extend an $H^1$ function to a slightly larger cube by reflection; for a boundary half-cube, reflect across the flat boundary first. A finite partition of unity reduces the manifold statement to these models. For the zero extension to a fixed large cube, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

$$
\|u(\cdot+h)-u\|_2\leq |h|\|\nabla u\|_2
\tag{7.3}
$$

away from the outer extension boundary, while a fixed cutoff makes the boundary strip uniformly small.

Partition the large cube into cubes of side $\delta$ and replace $u$ by its average on each small cube. The Poincare inequality on a cube, obtained by integrating differences along coordinate segments, gives

$$
\|u-P_\delta u\|_2\leq C\delta\|u\|_{H^1}.
$$

The range of $P_\delta$ is finite-dimensional. Thus the $H^1$ unit ball lies within $C\delta$ of a bounded set in a fixed finite-dimensional space and is totally bounded in $L^2$. Completeness of $L^2$ turns total boundedness into relative compactness. Returning through the finite atlas proves (7.2). $\square$

The same proof gives compactness $H^s(M)\hookrightarrow H^t(M)$ whenever $s>t$. Smoothness of the boundary can be weakened to the existence of bounded local extension maps, for example a Lipschitz boundary. We retain smooth boundary because truncation supplies it.

### 7.3 Compactness after imposing vanishing constant terms

Let $H^1_{\mathrm{cusp}}(X)$ be the closed subspace of $H^1(X)$ whose constant terms vanish in every cusp. Closedness follows because fiber averaging is bounded on each cusp cylinder and commutes with weak limits.

**Theorem 7.2.** Under the standard cusp hypothesis, the inclusion

$$
H^1_{\mathrm{cusp}}(X)\hookrightarrow L^2(X)
\tag{7.4}
$$

is compact.

**Proof.** Let $(u_n)$ be bounded in $H^1_{\mathrm{cusp}}(X)$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $T$ so large that (6.8), summed over the finitely many cusps, gives

$$
\|u_n\|_{L^2(X\setminus X_T)}<\varepsilon
$$

for every $n$. Restrictions to $X_T$ are bounded in $H^1(X_T)$, so Theorem 7.1 supplies a subsequence converging in $L^2(X_T)$. For two terms of this subsequence, the norm of their difference on the complement is at most $2\varepsilon$. A diagonal choice with $\varepsilon=2^{-k}$ produces a subsequence Cauchy in $L^2(X)$, hence convergent. $\square$

The proof reveals a general principle: compactness on every truncation plus uniform tightness of the $L^2$ mass is equivalent to global compactness for a bounded family.

### 7.4 Escape of mass and a counterexample

The full inclusion $H^1(X)\to L^2(X)$ is not compact when a cusp has a nonzero constant mode. In one end choose $\phi\in C_c^\infty((0,1))$ and set

$$
u_n(t,y)=c_ne^{\rho_ct}\phi(t-n),
$$

independent of $y$, with $c_n$ chosen so that $\|u_n\|_2=1$. On the support, the factor $e^{\rho_ct}$ cancels the density $e^{-2\rho_ct}$ in squared norm. The $t$ derivatives are uniformly bounded in $L^2$, and all transverse derivatives vanish. Thus $(u_n)$ is bounded in $H^1$. Its supports are disjoint for a spaced subsequence, so pairwise $L^2$ distances equal $\sqrt2$.

This counterexample is not a defect of finite volume or of the chosen norm. It is the geometric realization of a continuous spectral channel. Any proof claiming global Rellich compactness on the entire finite-volume quotient must have silently discarded these modes.

## 8. Continuous and cuspidal spectral directions

The compactness theorem has a spectral interpretation. Constant terms turn the cusp into a half-line and allow continuous propagation; transverse oscillation produces an expanding potential and confinement. We now make that contrast precise enough for later spectral decompositions.

### 8.1 The constant-term channel

In the model density $e^{-2\rho t}dt\,dy$, the map

$$
(Ua)(t)=e^{-\rho t}a(t)
$$

is unitary from the constant-mode space to $L^2([T_0,\infty),dt)$. The radial positive Laplacian

$$
-\partial_t^2+2\rho\partial_t
$$

is conjugated by $U$ to $-\partial_t^2+\rho^2$. Boundary behavior at $T_0$ changes at most the discrete part, while the half-line supports continuous spectrum $[\rho^2,\infty)$.

For completeness, take $\xi\geq0$ and long cutoffs

$$
v_L(t)=L^{-1/2}\chi((t-T_L)/L)e^{i\xi t},
$$

with $T_L\to\infty$ and $\|\chi\|_2=1$. Then $\|v_L\|_2=1$, $v_L$ converges weakly to zero, and

$$
\|(-\partial_t^2+\rho^2-(\xi^2+\rho^2))v_L\|_2\longrightarrow0.
$$

These approximate eigenvectors prove that the resolvent is not compact and exhibit every energy in the stated interval. Lower-order coefficients tending to limits do not change this conclusion; their error on supports moving to infinity tends to zero.

### 8.2 Typewise compact resolvent on the cuspidal space

Fix a finite level and a compact type, and let $\mathcal H_{\mathrm{cusp}}$ be the resulting closed cuspidal $L^2$ subspace. Let $P\geq I$ be a symmetric invariant elliptic operator whose closed quadratic-form domain on this subspace is $H^1_{\mathrm{cusp}}$ with equivalent norm. Theorem 7.2 makes the form-domain inclusion compact. The spectral theory of closed forms therefore gives:

**Theorem 8.1.** The self-adjoint operator associated with $P$ on $\mathcal H_{\mathrm{cusp}}$ has compact resolvent. Its spectrum consists of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity tending to infinity, and its eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal H_{\mathrm{cusp}}$.

Every weak eigenvector is smooth by Chapter 4 and rapidly decreasing by Section 6.4. The analytic outputs—discreteness, finite-dimensional spectral windows, smoothness, and decay—come from four distinct inputs: closed forms, cusp compactness, ellipticity, and vanishing constant terms.

### 8.3 Finite spectral windows and multiplicity

Let $K$ be the chosen maximal compact subgroup. Its isotypic projections commute with $P$ in the strong domain sense because right translations preserve the invariant form. For a type $\tau$ and $\Lambda_0<\infty$, the joint space

$$
\mathcal H(\tau;\Lambda_0)
=\mathbf 1_{[0,\Lambda_0]}(P)\mathcal H(\tau)
$$

is finite-dimensional. Every bounded normal symmetry commuting with $P$ and $K$ preserves this space, and commuting such symmetries admit a common orthonormal eigenbasis there.

This is the analytic origin of finite multiplicity. It does not say that an irreducible group representation is finite-dimensional. It says that only finitely many copies can contribute to one fixed compact type and one bounded elliptic window.

### 8.4 Why the assertions are typewise

A central invariant differential operator can act by one scalar on an entire infinite-dimensional irreducible representation. Its eigenspace on the unrestricted right regular space is then infinite-dimensional, so its resolvent cannot be compact there. Fixing a compact type cuts each relevant irreducible piece to finite dimension, and adding the compact-direction Laplacian makes the measuring operator elliptic.

Likewise, summing over infinitely many finite levels can destroy compactness by repeating the same bounded spectral window. The correct order is: fix finite level and compact type, prove compactness and finite windows, and only then sum over levels and types with quantitative trace-norm bounds.

## 9. Heat and resolvent smoothing

Compact resolvent turns the elliptic operator into a countable measuring device. Functional calculus then produces smoothing operators whose eigenvalue decay can be converted into regular kernels. The remaining issue is quantitative: compactness alone gives no trace, so we establish a polynomial counting bound.

### 9.1 Spectral smoothing estimates

Let $P\geq I$ be the cuspidal typewise operator of Chapter 8. For $r\geq0$ and $t>0$,

$$
\|P^re^{-tP}\|
=\sup_{\lambda\geq1}\lambda^re^{-t\lambda}
\leq C_rt^{-r}.
\tag{9.1}
$$

Consequently

$$
e^{-tP}:L^2\longrightarrow H^{2r}
$$

has norm at most $C_rt^{-r}$. Similarly,

$$
(P+z)^{-N}:H^s\longrightarrow H^{s+2N}
\tag{9.2}
$$

is bounded when $z$ stays in a closed sector avoiding the negative spectrum, with norm controlled by the distance of $-z$ from the spectrum. Elliptic norm equivalence translates these spectral estimates into invariant derivatives.

### 9.2 Polynomial eigenvalue bounds

**Proposition 9.1.** Under the standard cusp hypothesis, there are constants $C,D$ such that the counting function of $P$ on a fixed level and compact type satisfies

$$
N_P(\Lambda)\leq C(1+\Lambda)^D.
\tag{9.3}
$$

**Proof strategy.** A bounded spectral vector has controlled $H^1$ norm; the cusp estimate forces most of it into a truncation whose height grows only logarithmically with $\Lambda$. Finite-dimensional approximation on that truncation then bounds the dimension.

For $u$ in the spectral subspace $E_\Lambda=\mathbf1_{[1,\Lambda]}(P)\mathcal H$, form equivalence gives

$$
\|u\|_{H^1}\leq C\Lambda^{1/2}\|u\|_2.
$$

Choose $T=C_0+C_1\log(1+\Lambda)$ so that (6.8) makes

$$
\|u\|_{L^2(X\setminus X_T)}\leq\tfrac12\|u\|_2.
$$

Restriction to $X_T$ is therefore injective on $E_\Lambda$ and bounded below.

Cover the lifted truncated region by invariant coordinate balls and subdivide each into cubes of side $h=c\Lambda^{-1/2}$. Replacing a function by its average on every cube has error at most $Ch\|u\|_{H^1}\leq\frac14\|u\|_2$ when $c$ is small. Hence this finite-rank averaging map is injective on $E_\Lambda$. The number of cubes is at most $C e^{bT}h^{-d}$: exponential growth in $T$ is a crude bound obtained from the finite cusp atlas and (6.4). Thus

$$
\dim E_\Lambda\leq Ce^{bT}\Lambda^{d/2}
\leq C(1+\Lambda)^D
$$

for $D=d/2+bC_1$. This proves (9.3). $\square$

The exponent is not asserted to be optimal. Sharp asymptotics require finer geometry; polynomial growth is exactly what smoothing and trace arguments need.

### 9.3 Smooth kernels and their derivatives

Let $(\lambda_n,\phi_n)$ be an orthonormal eigenbasis. For $t>0$ set

$$
K_t(x,y)=\sum_ne^{-t\lambda_n}\phi_n(x)\overline{\phi_n(y)}.
\tag{9.4}
$$

By (4.5), local Sobolev embedding, and (9.3), every differentiated series converges uniformly on compact subsets of $X\times X$. Hence $K_t$ is smooth and is the integral kernel of $e^{-tP}$. In the cusps, (6.10) applied uniformly to eigenfunctions with the polynomial bounds (4.5) gives rapid decay of $K_t$ and all its derivatives after the exponential spectral factor is summed.

For resolvents,

$$
K_{N,z}(x,y)=\sum_n(\lambda_n+z)^{-N}
\phi_n(x)\overline{\phi_n(y)}
\tag{9.5}
$$

is smooth when $N$ is large enough relative to the desired number of derivatives and the exponent $D$. Smaller powers still improve Sobolev order but need not possess a pointwise continuous kernel.

### 9.4 Heat and resolvent traces

From (9.3), summation by dyadic intervals gives

$$
\sum_ne^{-t\lambda_n}<\infty
$$

for every $t>0$, and

$$
\sum_n(1+\lambda_n)^{-N}<\infty
\quad\text{whenever }N>D.
\tag{9.6}
$$

Thus $e^{-tP}$ and sufficiently high resolvent powers are trace class. Half-powers are Hilbert--Schmidt when their squared eigenvalues satisfy the same condition. The rapid cusp bounds justify the diagonal integral, and the factorization at time $t/2$ gives

$$
\operatorname{tr}(e^{-tP})
=\sum_ne^{-t\lambda_n}
=\int_XK_t(x,x)\,dx.
\tag{9.7}
$$

The last equality is not obtained by restricting an arbitrary square-integrable kernel to the diagonal. It follows from the Hilbert--Schmidt factorization

$$
e^{-tP}=e^{-tP/2}e^{-tP/2}
$$

and the canonical smooth representative (9.4).

### 9.5 Off-diagonal localization

For a Laplace-type $P\geq0$, heat cannot move significant mass an arbitrarily long distance in a short time. If $A,B\subset X$ are measurable with distance $r>0$, then

$$
\|\mathbf1_Ae^{-tP}\mathbf1_B\|_{2\to2}
\leq \exp\left(-\frac{r^2}{4ct}\right),
\tag{9.8}
$$

where $c$ is the principal-symbol speed constant.

To prove it, choose a bounded Lipschitz function $\psi$ equal to zero on $B$, at least $r$ on $A$, and with $|\nabla\psi|\leq1$. For $u(t)=e^{-tP}u_0$, differentiate $\|e^{a\psi}u(t)\|_2^2$. Integration by parts and the symbol bound give

$$
\frac d{dt}\|e^{a\psi}u(t)\|_2^2
\leq ca^2\|e^{a\psi}u(t)\|_2^2.
$$

Gronwall yields $\|e^{a\psi}u(t)\|_2\leq e^{ca^2t/2}\|u_0\|_2$. On $A$ this costs $e^{-ar}$; optimizing at $a=r/(ct)$ gives (9.8), with the displayed harmless normalization of $c$. Smooth approximations to $\psi$ justify the calculation. This estimate is useful when periodized kernels are split into near and far group elements.

## 10. Analytic estimates for automorphic kernels

An automorphic kernel is a periodization of a kernel on the covering group. Its formula is simple; its legitimacy is not. One must control orbit counting, differentiate the sum, exchange summation with integration, remove constant terms, and only then take a trace.

### 10.1 Periodized kernels and orbit counting

Let $k:G\to\mathbb C$ be smooth and define formally

$$
K_k(x,y)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}k(g^{-1}\gamma h),
\qquad x=\Gamma g,\quad y=\Gamma h.
\tag{10.1}
$$

The expression is independent of the chosen lifts after reindexing $\Gamma$. Let $\sigma:G\to[0,\infty)$ be a proper length satisfying $\sigma(ab)\leq\sigma(a)+\sigma(b)$. Assume the orbit-counting estimate

$$
\#\{\gamma:n\leq\sigma(g^{-1}\gamma h)<n+1\}
\leq C e^{bn}H(g)H(h)
\tag{10.2}
$$

for some nonnegative height function $H$. If every left-right derivative $Dk$ satisfies

$$
|Dk(z)|\leq C_{D,\varepsilon}e^{-(b+\varepsilon)\sigma(z)},
\tag{10.3}
$$

then the series for $DK_k$ converges absolutely and locally uniformly, with

$$
|DK_k(x,y)|\leq C_DH(g)H(h).
\tag{10.4}
$$

Indeed, group the sum into the shells in (10.2); the resulting geometric series is $\sum_ne^{-\varepsilon n}$. Local uniformity and the same estimate for all derivatives permit termwise differentiation. Compact support of $k$ gives pointwise finiteness, but not by itself an integrable uniform bound in a cusp; (10.2)--(10.4) explain the missing issue.

### 10.2 Differentiation, convolution, and Fubini

Let $T_k$ be the integral operator with kernel (10.1). If the nonnegative majorant in (10.4) is integrable against $|u(y)|$ for the class under consideration, unfolding a fundamental domain gives

$$
(T_ku)(\Gamma g)
=\int_Gk(g^{-1}h)\widetilde u(h)\,dh,
\tag{10.5}
$$

where $\widetilde u$ is the lifted invariant function. Tonelli applies first to absolute values; Fubini follows. Left derivatives in $x$ and right derivatives in $y$ land on corresponding derivatives of $k$, so (10.3) proves all smoothing estimates directly.

If $k=k_1*k_2$, absolute convergence and threefold Fubini give $T_k=T_{k_1}T_{k_2}$. This factorization is analytically valuable when both factors have square-integrable quotient kernels: their product is trace class, and the trace is computed by the two-kernel contraction without any premature diagonal restriction.

### 10.3 Cuspidal annihilation of constant terms

In a cusp associated with a compact transverse quotient $N_\Gamma\backslash N$, suppose the covering kernel has vanishing parabolic average

$$
\int_Nk(n g)\,dn=0
\tag{10.6}
$$

for every $g$, with Haar measure normalized compatibly with the quotient cross-section. Assume (10.3) strongly enough that all relevant sums and integrals are absolutely convergent. Averaging (10.1) over $N_\Gamma\backslash N$ and unfolding the lattice sum in the unipotent direction gives

$$
\mathcal C_c(K_k(\cdot,y))=0.
\tag{10.7}
$$

The decisive step is the disjoint decomposition of $N$ by $N_\Gamma$ translates; after reindexing, the quotient integral and lattice sum become the full integral in (10.6). Therefore $T_k$ lands in the cuspidal subspace in the first variable. Applying the analogous right average makes it annihilate noncuspidal input as well.

Equation (10.7) is not a consequence of smoothing or trace class. It is a structural cancellation of the chosen test kernel. Once proved, it activates the cusp decay and compactness theorems.

### 10.4 Trace-class criteria from elliptic smoothing

Let $P$ be the positive cuspidal typewise elliptic operator. Suppose that for some integer $N>D$,

$$
T_kP^N:L^2\longrightarrow L^2
$$

extends boundedly. This is equivalent to a smoothing estimate of order $2N$ for the adjoint kernel. Then

$$
T_k=(T_kP^N)P^{-N}
\tag{10.8}
$$

is trace class by (9.6). A symmetric alternative is to prove $T_kP^{N/2}$ and $P^{N/2}T_k$ bounded with $N>D$; this factors $T_k$ through two Hilbert--Schmidt half-powers.

If its canonical smooth kernel has the rapid cusp bound supplied by (10.7) and Chapter 6, then

$$
\operatorname{tr}(T_k)
=\int_XK_k(x,x)\,dx.
\tag{10.9}
$$

On an orthonormal cuspidal eigenbasis, the same trace is an absolutely convergent spectral sum. Absolute convergence permits regrouping by compact type or other commuting symmetries. Summing over all types requires

$$
\sum_\tau\|T_k|_{\mathcal H(\tau)}\|_1<\infty;
\tag{10.10}
$$

typewise trace class alone is not enough.

### 10.5 A kernel estimate ledger

The estimates needed in later kernel arguments can now be checked in a fixed order.

1. **Pointwise definition:** properness or orbit counting makes (10.1) meaningful.
2. **Derivative convergence:** (10.2)--(10.3) give local uniform convergence of every differentiated series.
3. **Operator boundedness:** a Schur bound or unfolding estimate controls integration in $y$.
4. **Cuspidal range:** the parabolic average (10.6), with absolute convergence, proves (10.7).
5. **Tail control:** vanishing constant terms and invariant derivative bounds give (6.9)--(6.10).
6. **Trace ideal:** factorization or (10.8) proves trace class before a diagonal is used.
7. **Diagonal integration:** the canonical smooth rapidly decreasing kernel justifies (10.9).
8. **Global type sum:** the numerical estimate (10.10) permits reordering the complete spectral expansion.

Omitting any one of these steps creates a recognizable error: a merely formal series, an unjustified derivative, an invalid interchange, an escaping constant term, or a diagonal with no operator-theoretic meaning.

## 11. A global regularity and compactness theorem

The preceding chapters can be condensed into one reusable theorem. Its length lies in the hypotheses, which prevent hidden assumptions about torsion, measure, ends, boundaries, and domains.

### 11.1 Hypotheses and statement

Let $G$ be a second-countable unimodular real Lie group, $\Gamma\subset G$ a torsion-free lattice, and $X=\Gamma\backslash G$ with the quotient of a left-invariant complete metric and the finite right-invariant measure (1.1). Suppose $X$ satisfies the finite standard cusp hypothesis (6.1)--(6.4).

Let $E\to X$ be a finite-rank Hermitian homogeneous bundle. Fix a finite level and compact type. Let $\mathcal H_{\mathrm{cusp}}\subset L^2(X;E)$ be a closed invariant subspace whose vectors have zero transverse projection in every proper cusp. Let $P$ be a symmetric second-order operator on compactly supported smooth cuspidal sections such that:

- its coefficients and all invariant derivatives are uniformly bounded;
- its principal symbol is uniformly strongly elliptic;
- $P\geq I$ on its initial domain;
- its closed form norm is equivalent to the $H^1$ norm on $H^1_{\mathrm{cusp}}(X;E)$.

**Theorem 11.1.** Under these hypotheses:

1. $P$ has a unique self-adjoint realization associated with its closed form, and its operator domain is $H^2(X;E)\cap\mathcal H_{\mathrm{cusp}}$.
2. For every integer $s\geq0$,
   $$
   \|u\|_{H^{s+2}}\leq C_s(\|Pu\|_{H^s}+\|u\|_2).
   $$
3. A weak solution of $Pu=f$ is locally two derivatives smoother than $f$; smooth $f$ gives smooth $u$.
4. The form-domain inclusion into $\mathcal H_{\mathrm{cusp}}$ is compact, so $P$ has compact resolvent.
5. Every eigenvector is a smooth vector and is rapidly decreasing with all invariant derivatives in every cusp.
6. The eigenvalue counting function is polynomially bounded. Hence heat operators and sufficiently high resolvent powers are trace class and possess smooth rapidly decreasing kernels with valid diagonal trace formulas.

### 11.2 Proof of the theorem

The closed positive form gives the self-adjoint realization. The global estimate of Theorem 3.2, extended to uniformly controlled coefficients, identifies its graph domain with $H^2$ exactly as in Theorem 3.3. Commuting derivatives through $P$ proves assertion 2. Difference quotients and localization prove assertion 3.

The fiber Poincare inequality gives the uniform tail estimate (6.8). Rellich compactness on $X_T$ and the tail estimate give compactness of the form embedding by Theorem 7.2. Closed-form spectral theory then gives compact resolvent. Elliptic bootstrapping makes eigenvectors globally Sobolev of every order; Theorem 5.1 makes them smooth vectors, and Section 6.4 gives rapid decay.

Finally, the logarithmic truncation and finite-rank averaging argument prove the polynomial counting bound. Spectral functional calculus gives the heat and resolvent smoothing estimates; the counting bound gives trace summability; Sobolev embedding gives smooth kernels; cusp decay gives diagonal integrability; and Hilbert--Schmidt factorization proves the diagonal trace identity. Every conclusion has now been attached to the hypothesis that produces it. $\square$

### 11.3 Consequences for spectral kernels

Suppose a smooth convolution kernel preserves the fixed level and type, satisfies the derivative bounds of Section 10.1, and has vanishing proper parabolic averages. It maps $L^2$ into the domain of every power of $P$ by (5.5), lands in $\mathcal H_{\mathrm{cusp}}$ by (10.7), and is trace class by (10.8) with $N>D$. Its trace is simultaneously

$$
\sum_n\langle T_k\phi_n,\phi_n\rangle
\quad\text{and}\quad
\int_XK_k(x,x)\,dx,
$$

both absolutely convergent. This is the precise analytic bridge from a periodized geometric kernel to a cuspidal spectral expansion.

The theorem does not classify the cusps, construct the constant-term decomposition, or identify the irreducible spectral labels. Those are global structural tasks. It supplies the analytic engine they require once the reduction data and the chosen cuspidal test factor are known.

## 12. Final synthesis

Invariant Sobolev theory begins with a simple compatibility. The curves $x\exp(tX)$ both differentiate the right group action and define a global frame on the left quotient. Unimodularity makes their derivatives skew-adjoint in the quotient measure. Completeness supplies cutoffs with vanishing gradient and thereby prevents an unrecorded boundary condition at infinity. These facts turn the positive sum of squares into a canonical self-adjoint elliptic measuring operator.

Ellipticity is local but its consequences are global. Freezing the principal symbol and using Fourier estimates controls all derivatives on a coordinate ball. Translation on the covering group makes the constants uniform even when quotient injectivity radii shrink. Difference quotients transfer the estimate from smooth functions to weak solutions. Iteration shows that smooth data produce smooth solutions, while the invariant Sobolev scale identifies global smooth vectors as the common domain of all powers of one positive elliptic operator.

Noncompactness enters through one sharply defined obstruction. In a cusp, the transverse zero mode sees a half-line and supports approximate eigenvectors moving outward. All nonzero transverse modes feel derivatives expanding like $e^{\lambda t}$. Vanishing of the constant term removes the zero mode; the compact-fiber Poincare inequality then gains $e^{-\lambda t}$ on every application. This produces uniform $L^2$ tails and, for smooth vectors, rapid pointwise decay with every derivative.

Truncation separates the two compactness mechanisms. On the compact manifold with boundary $X_T$, finite-dimensional averaging proves Rellich compactness without imposing a boundary condition. Beyond $X_T$, the cusp estimate makes every bounded cuspidal energy family uniformly small. Together they give compact embedding and therefore compact resolvent on each fixed level and compact type. The full space remains noncompact for the correct reason: constant terms and unrestricted types carry genuine continuous or infinitely repeated directions.

Finally, heat and resolvent calculus convert regularity into kernels. Polynomial spectral counting makes sufficient smoothing trace class. Sobolev estimates select a canonical smooth kernel, cusp decay makes its diagonal integrable, and half-power factorization validates the trace formula. For periodized kernels, orbit counting, derivative decay, absolute unfolding, parabolic cancellation, elliptic factorization, and type summability form a complete checklist.

The resulting analytic architecture is therefore exact:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\text{complete invariant geometry}+\text{ellipticity}
\Longrightarrow \text{global regularity},\\
\text{zero constant terms}+\text{cusp scaling}
\Longrightarrow \text{tightness and compactness},\\
\text{compact typewise spectrum}+\text{smoothing}
\Longrightarrow \text{legitimate automorphic traces}.
\end{gathered}}
$$

Every qualification in this chain has a purpose. Boundary conditions belong to truncations, not to the complete quotient. Finite volume does not replace cusp decay. Positivity does not replace ellipticity. Smoothness does not replace trace class. Typewise compactness does not imply unrestricted compactness. With these distinctions in place, the analytic estimates needed for cuspidal spectral theory and automorphic kernels follow from one coherent Sobolev and elliptic framework.
