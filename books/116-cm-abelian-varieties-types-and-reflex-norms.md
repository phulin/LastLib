## 1. Why complex multiplication has a type

An abelian variety of dimension $g$ can have at most $2g$ independent commuting rational endomorphisms. Complex multiplication is the extremal case: a commutative semisimple algebra of dimension $2g$ acts. Yet the algebra alone does not determine the complex geometry. Each complex embedding of that algebra must be assigned either to the holomorphic tangent space or to its antiholomorphic conjugate. The resulting choice is the **CM type**.

That choice controls almost every later construction. It specifies the complex torus attached to an ideal, decides which alternating trace forms are positive, determines a field of definition for the Hodge data, and produces a norm map in the direction opposite to the type norm. The purpose of this book is to build that package carefully enough that no inverse, conjugate, or polarization factor is left implicit.

### 1.1 The organizing problem

Suppose a number field $K$ of degree $2g$ acts on a complex abelian variety $A$ of dimension $g$. Since

$$
H_1(A,\mathbf Q)
$$

has dimension $2g$ over $\mathbf Q$, it is one-dimensional over $K$. After tensoring with $\mathbf C$, the action splits into its embedding eigenspaces:

$$
H_1(A,\mathbf Q)\otimes_{\mathbf Q}\mathbf C
 =\bigoplus_{\varphi:K\hookrightarrow\mathbf C}V_\varphi,
\qquad \dim_{\mathbf C}V_\varphi=1.
$$

The complex structure partitions these lines between bidegrees $(-1,0)$ and $(0,-1)$. Complex conjugation interchanges the two. Thus exactly one embedding from each conjugate pair occurs in one chosen half. This is why a CM type is not auxiliary decoration: it is the eigenvalue record of the Hodge structure.

Three problems then become inseparable.

First, which lattices in $K$ give algebraic, rather than merely complex, tori? Second, how does one express a polarization entirely in ideal-theoretic terms? Third, what arithmetic field and norm are forced by the embedding choice? Chapters 2--8 solve the first two; Chapters 9--11 solve the structural part of the third.

### 1.2 Standing conventions

All number fields are subfields of a fixed algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbf Q}\subset\mathbf C$. If $K$ is a CM field, its distinguished complex conjugation is denoted $c$ and $\bar x=c(x)$. We write

$$
\Sigma_K=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf Q}(K,\mathbf C).
$$

A CM type $\Phi$ is viewed as a subset of $\Sigma_K$, not as its characteristic function unless explicitly stated. We use homology Hodge type

$$
H_1(A,\mathbf C)=H^{-1,0}\oplus H^{0,-1};
$$

the type $\Phi$ labels the eigenlines in $H^{-1,0}$, equivalently the eigenvalues on $\operatorname{Lie}A$. This convention fixes the sign of the Hodge cocharacter in Chapter 9.

For a fractional ideal $\mathfrak a$ of $K$, the lattice $\Phi(\mathfrak a)$ means its diagonal image in $\mathbf C^\Phi$. The alternating form attached to $\xi\in K$ will be

$$
E_\xi(x,y)=\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbf Q}(\xi\bar x y). \tag{1.1}
$$

With this order of the variables, positivity is

$$
\operatorname{Im}\varphi(\xi)>0\qquad(\varphi\in\Phi). \tag{1.2}
$$

Reversing $x$ and $y$, or using $\operatorname{Tr}(\xi x\bar y)$, reverses the sign condition. We will not alternate between those conventions.

The reflex norm will be denoted

$$
r_\Phi:\operatorname{Res}_{E/\mathbf Q}\mathbf G_m
\longrightarrow \operatorname{Res}_{K/\mathbf Q}\mathbf G_m, \tag{1.3}
$$

where $E=E(K,\Phi)$ is the reflex field. Thus on rational points it goes from $E^\times$ to $K^\times$. The elementary type norm goes the other way, from $K^\times$ to $E^\times$. Keeping both arrows visible is the simplest protection against a variance error.

### 1.3 From extra endomorphisms to a torus

Multiplication makes $K^\times$ a group of rational automorphisms of the one-dimensional $K$-space $H_1(A,\mathbf Q)$. Algebraically this group is the restriction-of-scalars torus

$$
T_K=\operatorname{Res}_{K/\mathbf Q}\mathbf G_m.
$$

Over $\mathbf C$ it is a product of one copy of $\mathbf G_m$ for each embedding of $K$. The complex structure is encoded by a cocharacter that is $z$ in the coordinates indexed by $\Phi$ and $1$ in the conjugate coordinates. The smallest field over which the conjugacy class of that cocharacter is defined is the reflex field. The smallest rational subtorus containing it is the Mumford--Tate torus.

This torus viewpoint does not replace ideals. It organizes them. Rational points give elements, finite adelic points give local multipliers, integral orbits give fractional ideals, and the cocharacter determines the monomial exponents in the reflex norm. We will repeatedly move among these four descriptions and prove that the arrows agree.

### 1.4 The boundary of this book

This volume develops the algebraic and complex-analytic structure that precedes the main theorem of complex multiplication. It constructs CM abelian varieties, classifies them over $\mathbf C$, describes their ideal actions and polarizations, and defines the reflex field and reflex norm on elements, tori, ideals, and finite ideles.

It does **not** identify the Galois action on CM points with an ideal action through global reciprocity. Such an assertion requires a theorem comparing two actions that are constructed by entirely different means. Nor do we infer canonical models, good reduction, or finite-place Frobenius formulas from the existence of the reflex norm. Those are reciprocity and reduction results, not formal consequences of the definitions. Chapter 12 will state the exact interface without crossing it.

## 2. CM fields and positive involutions

The word “complex” in complex multiplication is arithmetic, not merely analytic. The endomorphism field must have no real embeddings, but it must still contain a real half of exactly half the degree. This chapter isolates that field structure and the positivity cone that later turns trace pairings into polarizations.

### 2.1 Totally real fields and CM extensions

A number field $F$ is **totally real** if every embedding $F\hookrightarrow\mathbf C$ has image in $\mathbf R$. A number field $K$ is a **CM field** if it is a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real field $F$. Thus

$$
[K:F]=2,\qquad [F:\mathbf Q]=g,\qquad [K:\mathbf Q]=2g.
$$

The nontrivial automorphism of $K/F$ is denoted $c$. Under every complex embedding it becomes ordinary complex conjugation:

$$
\varphi(\bar x)=\overline{\varphi(x)}. \tag{2.1}
$$

To prove (2.1), choose $u\in K$ with $u^2\in F$ and $K=F(u)$. Because $K$ has no real embedding, every real embedding $\tau:F\hookrightarrow\mathbf R$ sends $u^2$ to a negative real number. The two extensions of $\tau$ to $K$ send $u$ to opposite purely imaginary square roots. They are exchanged both by $c$ and by ordinary complex conjugation.

The fixed field of $c$ is $K^+=F$. Every embedding of $K$ comes in a pair $\{\varphi,\bar\varphi\}$, where $\bar\varphi=\varphi\circ c$. There are exactly $g$ pairs.

### 2.2 Equivalent descriptions of a CM field

The definition above has two useful intrinsic forms.

**Proposition 2.1.** For a number field $K$ of degree $2g$, the following are equivalent.

1. $K$ is a CM field.
2. There is a nonidentity involution $c$ of $K$ such that $\varphi\circ c=\overline\varphi$ for every $\varphi\in\Sigma_K$.
3. There is a nonidentity involution $c$ such that

   $$
   \operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbf Q}(x\bar x)>0
   $$

   for every nonzero $x\in K$.

**Proof.** We proved $1\Rightarrow2$. Under condition 2,

$$
\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbf Q}(x\bar x)
=\sum_{\varphi\in\Sigma_K}|\varphi(x)|^2>0,
$$

so $2\Rightarrow3$.

Assume 3. Extend $c$ to the factors of $K\otimes\mathbf R$. The involution permutes the archimedean factors in orbits of size one or two. A two-element orbit contributes either $2xy$ on two real factors or $4\operatorname{Re}(zw)$ on two complex factors; both forms are indefinite. Thus every factor is fixed. A fixed real factor would mean that a real embedding $\tau$ satisfies $\tau\circ c=\tau$, and injectivity of $\tau$ would force $c=1$, contrary to the hypothesis. On a fixed complex factor the identity involution gives $2\operatorname{Re}(z^2)$, which is indefinite, whereas complex conjugation gives $2|z|^2$. Positivity therefore rules out real factors and forces complex conjugation on every complex factor. The fixed algebra is $\mathbf R^g$, so the fixed field is totally real of degree $g$, $K$ has no real embeddings, and $K$ is quadratic over its fixed field. Thus $3\Rightarrow1$. $\square$

The positivity in condition 3 is often called positivity of the involution. It is the field-theoretic shadow of the Rosati involution attached to a polarization.

### 2.3 CM algebras and conjugation

PEL constructions sometimes use products rather than fields. A **CM algebra** is a finite product

$$
K=\prod_{i=1}^rK_i
$$

of CM fields, equipped with componentwise conjugation. Its degree is the sum of the degrees of its factors. The fixed algebra $K^+=\prod_iK_i^+$ is totally real. Its embeddings are the disjoint union of the embeddings of the factors.

Every assertion based only on embeddings, traces, conjugation, and restriction of scalars extends componentwise to CM algebras. Assertions involving a fractional ideal then mean a product of fractional ideals. Assertions about simplicity do not: idempotents in a product algebra split any abelian variety on which they act, at least up to isogeny. We develop the field case first and point out the product modification when it matters.

The center of a semisimple endomorphism algebra with a positive involution can have totally real and CM factors. Complex multiplication occupies the case in which the relevant commutative algebra has total dimension $2g$ and its positive involution is CM conjugation. Real multiplication, by contrast, has only $g$ dimensions and does not by itself split the tangent space into conjugate halves.

### 2.4 Positive imaginary elements

Put

$$
K^-=\{x\in K:\bar x=-x\}.
$$

It is a one-dimensional vector space over $K^+$. For a type $\Phi$, define the open cone

$$
K^-_{\Phi,+}
=\{\xi\in K^-:\operatorname{Im}\varphi(\xi)>0
\text{ for every }\varphi\in\Phi\}. \tag{2.2}
$$

This cone is nonempty.

**Lemma 2.2.** For every CM type $\Phi$ there exists $\xi\in K$ with $\bar\xi=-\xi$ and $\operatorname{Im}\varphi(\xi)>0$ for all $\varphi\in\Phi$.

**Proof.** Choose any nonzero $\delta\in K^-$; for example $\delta=a-\bar a$ with $a\notin K^+$. For each real embedding $\tau:K^+\hookrightarrow\mathbf R$, choose the member $\varphi\in\Phi$ above it. The number $\operatorname{Im}\varphi(\delta)$ is nonzero, because $\varphi(\delta)$ is purely imaginary and $\delta\ne0$ under every embedding. Prescribe a sign for $u\in K^+$ at $\tau$ so that

$$
\tau(u)\operatorname{Im}\varphi(\delta)>0.
$$

Weak approximation in the totally real field $K^+$ produces $u$ with all those signs. Then $\xi=u\delta$ has the required properties. $\square$

Multiplication by a totally positive element of $K^+$ preserves the cone. Multiplication by $-1$ interchanges the cones for $\Phi$ and $\bar\Phi$. This elementary sign geometry is exactly what the Riemann bilinear relations will detect.

## 3. CM types

A CM field supplies $g$ conjugate pairs of embeddings. A type chooses one member of every pair. That simple definition carries three different meanings: a list of tangent eigenvalues, a Hodge cocharacter, and an orientation of the imaginary cone. This chapter develops the first two and separates primitive types from those inherited from smaller CM fields.

### 3.1 Choosing half the embeddings

A **CM type** on a CM field $K$ is a subset $\Phi\subset\Sigma_K$ satisfying

$$
\Phi\cap\bar\Phi=\varnothing,
\qquad
\Phi\cup\bar\Phi=\Sigma_K. \tag{3.1}
$$

Thus $|\Phi|=g$. We also write $(K,\Phi)$ for a CM pair. Its conjugate type is $\bar\Phi=\{\bar\varphi:\varphi\in\Phi\}$.

The **type trace** and **type norm** are initially the complex-valued functions

$$
\operatorname{Tr}_\Phi(a)=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\varphi(a),
\qquad
N_\Phi(a)=\prod_{\varphi\in\Phi}\varphi(a). \tag{3.2}
$$

They should not be confused with the field trace and norm. Their sums with their conjugates recover the ordinary invariants:

$$
\operatorname{Tr}_\Phi(a)+\overline{\operatorname{Tr}_\Phi(a)}
=\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbf Q}(a),
$$

$$
N_\Phi(a)\,\overline{N_\Phi(a)}=N_{K/\mathbf Q}(a). \tag{3.3}
$$

Later, the stabilizer of the type will imply that the values in (3.2) lie in the reflex field.

### 3.2 Hodge structures of CM type

Let $V$ be a one-dimensional vector space over $K$. Then

$$
V_{\mathbf C}=V\otimes_{\mathbf Q}\mathbf C
\simeq\bigoplus_{\varphi\in\Sigma_K}V_\varphi,
$$

where $a\in K$ acts on $V_\varphi$ by $\varphi(a)$. Given $\Phi$, set

$$
V^{-1,0}=\bigoplus_{\varphi\in\Phi}V_\varphi,
\qquad
V^{0,-1}=\bigoplus_{\varphi\in\bar\Phi}V_\varphi. \tag{3.4}
$$

Complex conjugation interchanges the two sums, so (3.4) is a rational Hodge structure of weight $-1$. Conversely, any weight $-1$ Hodge structure on the rank-one $K$-module $V$ for which $K$ acts by Hodge endomorphisms must have this form: each one-dimensional eigenspace lies in one bidegree, and its conjugate lies in the other.

This proves the basic uniqueness statement.

**Proposition 3.1.** A CM type on $K$ is equivalent to a weight $-1$ Hodge structure on the rational vector space $K$ for which multiplication by $K$ consists of Hodge endomorphisms and each embedding eigenspace has multiplicity one.

No positivity is included in Proposition 3.1. A rational Hodge structure of type $(-1,0)+(0,-1)$ comes from a complex torus, but it is polarizable only after an alternating form satisfying the Riemann relations is supplied. The cone from Lemma 2.2 will provide one.

### 3.3 Galois transport, equivalence, and stabilizers

For $\gamma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbf C/\mathbf Q)$, define

$$
\gamma\Phi=\{\gamma\circ\varphi:\varphi\in\Phi\}. \tag{3.5}
$$

Here embeddings retain domain $K$, so this is again a CM type on $K$: in a Galois CM field containing the normal closure of $K$, complex conjugation is central, and postcomposition therefore preserves conjugate pairs. To transport the embedded field itself to $\gamma(K)$, one instead conjugates both the domain and the embeddings. Two CM pairs $(K,\Phi)$ and $(K',\Phi')$ are **isomorphic** if there is a field isomorphism $u:K\to K'$ such that

$$
\Phi=\{\varphi'\circ u:\varphi'\in\Phi'\}.
$$

This is the equivalence relevant to unlabelled CM abelian varieties. If the embedding of $K$ into the endomorphism algebra is part of the data, one must retain the labelled type and may not quotient by $\operatorname{Aut}(K)$.

The stabilizer

$$
\Gamma_\Phi
=\{\gamma\in\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbf Q}/\mathbf Q):
\gamma\Phi=\Phi\} \tag{3.6}
$$

is open: the type is determined inside a finite Galois closure of $K$. Its fixed field will be the reflex field. No assertion that $\gamma$ preserves the distinguished embedded image of $K$ is implicit here; the action is by postcomposition on $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf Q}(K,\mathbf C)$.

### 3.4 Induced and primitive types

Let $K_0\subset K$ be an inclusion of CM fields. A type $\Phi_0$ on $K_0$ induces the type

$$
\operatorname{Ind}_{K_0}^{K}(\Phi_0)
=\{\varphi\in\Sigma_K:\varphi|_{K_0}\in\Phi_0\}. \tag{3.7}
$$

Indeed, each conjugate pair over an embedding of $K^+_0$ restricts to one of the conjugate embeddings of $K_0$, so (3.7) selects exactly one member of every pair. A type is **primitive** if it is not induced from a proper CM subfield.

The adjective concerns the pair, not merely the field. A CM field may support both primitive and imprimitive types. Induction is visible on Hodge structures: as a $K_0$-module, $K$ has rank $[K:K_0]$, and (3.4) is the direct sum of that many copies of the Hodge structure of $(K_0,\Phi_0)$. Consequently the associated complex abelian variety is isogenous to a power of the lower-dimensional one. Chapter 7 will prove the converse and thereby relate primitive types to simplicity.

For a CM algebra $\prod K_i$, a type is a disjoint union of types $\Phi_i$ on its factors. The corresponding Hodge structure and abelian variety split as products. Thus primitive types are necessarily field types.

### 3.5 Examples in degrees two and four

If $K$ is imaginary quadratic, $\Sigma_K=\{\varphi,\bar\varphi\}$ and there are two types, exchanged by conjugation. Once $K\subset\mathbf C$ is fixed, the usual type is $\{\operatorname{id}\}$. It yields the familiar elliptic curve $\mathbf C/\mathfrak a$ for a fractional ideal $\mathfrak a$.

Let $K=\mathbf Q(\zeta_5)$. Its embeddings are

$$
\sigma_a(\zeta_5)=\zeta_5^a,
\qquad a\in(\mathbf Z/5\mathbf Z)^\times,
$$

and conjugation sends $a$ to $-a$. Hence $\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2\}$ is a CM type, as are the three other choices of one member from each pair $\{1,4\}$ and $\{2,3\}$. The types $\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2\}$ and $\{\sigma_1,\sigma_3\}$ are conjugate under $\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbf Q)$, so they give isomorphic unlabelled CM data after transport. Since $K$ has no proper imaginary quadratic subfield, all its types are primitive.

By contrast, if $K=K_1K_2$ is a biquadratic CM field containing an imaginary quadratic field $K_1$, the type consisting of all embeddings restricting to a chosen embedding of $K_1$ is induced from $K_1$. The resulting abelian surface is isogenous to a square of a CM elliptic curve. Merely having a quartic endomorphism field therefore does not force simplicity.

## 4. Orders, lattices, and ideal arithmetic

The analytic construction needs a lattice stable under endomorphisms. Maximal orders are sufficient for many examples but too restrictive for moduli and isogeny questions: an isogenous variety can have a smaller endomorphism ring. This chapter develops the exact order-and-ideal language required to retain that information.

### 4.1 CM orders and multiplier rings

An **order** in a number field $K$ is a subring $\mathcal O\subset K$ containing $1$ and free of rank $[K:\mathbf Q]$ over $\mathbf Z$. A **CM order** is an order stable under conjugation. Its conductor in $\mathcal O_K$ is

$$
\mathfrak f=\{x\in\mathcal O_K:x\mathcal O_K\subseteq\mathcal O\}; \tag{4.1}
$$

it is the largest $\mathcal O_K$-ideal contained in $\mathcal O$ and is stable under conjugation.

A full lattice $\mathfrak a\subset K$ is a finitely generated free $\mathbf Z$-submodule spanning $K$ over $\mathbf Q$. Its **multiplier ring** is

$$
(\mathfrak a:\mathfrak a)=\{x\in K:x\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak a\}. \tag{4.2}
$$

This is an order. We call $\mathfrak a$ a proper fractional $\mathcal O$-ideal when its multiplier ring is exactly $\mathcal O$. Properness is essential: stability under $\mathcal O$ only gives an inclusion $\mathcal O\subseteq(\mathfrak a:\mathfrak a)$ and would forget extra endomorphisms.

Every full lattice becomes an integral ideal after multiplication by a nonzero integer. Conversely every nonzero finitely generated $\mathcal O$-submodule spanning $K$ is a full lattice. These elementary denominator clearings let one pass freely between analytic lattices and fractional ideals.

### 4.2 Trace duals and differents

For a full lattice $M\subset K$, define its trace dual

$$
M^t=\{x\in K:\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbf Q}(xM)\subseteq\mathbf Z\}. \tag{4.3}
$$

The trace pairing is nondegenerate because number fields are separable, so $M^t$ is again a full lattice and $(M^t)^t=M$. For the maximal order,

$$
\mathcal O_K^t=\mathfrak D_K^{-1}, \tag{4.4}
$$

the inverse different. If $\mathfrak a$ is an invertible fractional $\mathcal O_K$-ideal, then

$$
\mathfrak a^t=\mathfrak D_K^{-1}\mathfrak a^{-1}. \tag{4.5}
$$

Indeed, $x\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak D_K^{-1}$ is equivalent to $x\in\mathfrak D_K^{-1}\mathfrak a^{-1}$. For a nonmaximal order or a noninvertible ideal, the safe formula is the colon-lattice identity

$$
\mathfrak a^t=(\mathcal O^t:\mathfrak a)
=\{x\in K:x\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathcal O^t\}; \tag{4.6}
$$

one must not replace the colon by an inverse without invertibility.

Conjugation preserves the different of a CM field. Hence

$$
\overline{M^t}=(\bar M)^t. \tag{4.7}
$$

This identity will make the dual lattice of a CM trace pairing explicit.

### 4.3 Invertible ideals and ideal classes

A fractional $\mathcal O$-ideal $\mathfrak a$ is **invertible** if there is a fractional ideal $\mathfrak b$ with $\mathfrak a\mathfrak b=\mathcal O$. Then $\mathfrak b$ is unique and denoted $\mathfrak a^{-1}$. Invertible ideals are proper and locally free of rank one over $\mathcal O$. For the maximal order every nonzero fractional ideal is invertible; for a nonmaximal order this can fail at primes dividing the conductor.

The group of invertible fractional ideals modulo principal ideals is the Picard group

$$
\operatorname{Pic}(\mathcal O).
$$

Two proper invertible ideals $\mathfrak a$ and $\mathfrak b$ define the same class precisely when $\mathfrak b=x\mathfrak a$ for some $x\in K^\times$. The inverse convention in the ideal action of Chapter 5 will turn this group into a genuine left action on isomorphism classes.

Conjugation acts on $\operatorname{Pic}(\mathcal O)$. The product $\mathfrak a\bar{\mathfrak a}$ is a conjugation-stable fractional ideal of $K$, but its class need not match the class required by the trace dual. One should not silently regard every stable ideal as the extension of an ideal of $K^+$: ramified primes can obstruct such a literal descent. The class comparison inside $K$ is the global obstruction appearing in the principal-polarization equation.

### 4.4 Changing the order

If $\mathcal O\subseteq\mathcal O'\subseteq K$, extension sends an invertible $\mathcal O$-ideal prime to the conductor to an invertible $\mathcal O'$-ideal:

$$
\mathfrak a\longmapsto\mathfrak a\mathcal O'.
$$

Contraction is inverse away from conductor primes. At a conductor prime, extension can identify distinct ideals and a proper ideal can cease to be invertible. Thus ideal-class actions at nonmaximal level are cleanest when their ideals are prime to the conductor.

For any full lattice $\mathfrak a$, enlarging its multiplier ring enlarges the endomorphism order of the associated torus. The lattice $\mathcal O'$ contains $\mathcal O$ with finite index and produces an isogeny

$$
\mathbf C^\Phi/\Phi(\mathcal O)
\longrightarrow
\mathbf C^\Phi/\Phi(\mathcal O').
$$

This elementary example explains why endomorphism rings jump upward under isogeny and why a moduli problem that specifies an order must impose the action, not merely the rational field.

## 5. The analytic construction

We now turn the abstract Hodge structure into a complex torus. The formula is short—embed an ideal diagonally and divide—but its consequences are substantial. It makes ideals into lattices, multiplication into endomorphisms, ideal containment into isogenies, and ideal classes into isomorphism classes.

### 5.1 The type embedding and its lattices

For a CM pair $(K,\Phi)$ define

$$
\Phi_K:K\longrightarrow\mathbf C^\Phi,
\qquad
x\longmapsto(\varphi(x))_{\varphi\in\Phi}. \tag{5.1}
$$

As a map of $\mathbf Q$-vector spaces into a real vector space, $\Phi_K$ is an isomorphism

$$
K\otimes_{\mathbf Q}\mathbf R\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbf C^\Phi. \tag{5.2}
$$

To see this, the usual archimedean decomposition gives one complex factor for every conjugate pair. Choosing $\Phi$ chooses one coordinate from each pair. Both sides have real dimension $2g$, and injectivity follows because a field embedding never kills a nonzero element.

Consequently, for every full lattice $\mathfrak a\subset K$, the subgroup $\Phi_K(\mathfrak a)$ is discrete and cocompact in $\mathbf C^\Phi$. We define the complex torus

$$
A(\mathfrak a,\Phi)
=\mathbf C^\Phi/\Phi_K(\mathfrak a). \tag{5.3}
$$

It has dimension $g$. Multiplication by $a\in K$ acts diagonally on the universal cover:

$$
(z_\varphi)_\varphi\longmapsto
(\varphi(a)z_\varphi)_\varphi. \tag{5.4}
$$

It descends to an endomorphism precisely when $a\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak a$. Hence (4.2) gives

$$
\operatorname{End}_K(A(\mathfrak a,\Phi))
=(\mathfrak a:\mathfrak a), \tag{5.5}
$$

where the left side means endomorphisms induced by $K$-linear maps on the universal cover. We will show below that every endomorphism commuting with the full $K$-action has this form.

### 5.2 The complex torus and its endomorphisms

At this stage (5.3) is only a compact complex Lie group. To prove it is an abelian variety we need a positive integral Riemann form. Choose $\xi\in K^-_{\Phi,+}$ by Lemma 2.2. Since $\mathfrak a$ is finitely generated, there is a positive integer $m$ such that

$$
m\xi\,\bar{\mathfrak a}\mathfrak a
\subseteq\mathfrak D_K^{-1}. \tag{5.6}
$$

Chapter 6 proves that $E_{m\xi}$ from (1.1) is an integral positive Riemann form on $\Phi(\mathfrak a)$. The Riemann existence theorem therefore makes (5.3) projective. Thus:

**Theorem 5.1.** For every CM pair $(K,\Phi)$ and full lattice $\mathfrak a\subset K$, the torus $A(\mathfrak a,\Phi)$ is an abelian variety. Its multiplier order acts by algebraic endomorphisms.

The last assertion follows because a holomorphic homomorphism between polarized complex tori is algebraic. Concretely, its graph is a closed complex subtorus of the product; restriction of a product ample line bundle makes the graph projective, and the analytic graph is therefore algebraic.

Now suppose $f$ is a holomorphic endomorphism of $A(\mathfrak a,\Phi)$ commuting with $K$. Its derivative lifts to a complex-linear map of $\mathbf C^\Phi$. Commuting with the diagonal action of $K$ forces preservation of each distinct embedding eigenspace, so it is diagonal, say with entries $u_\varphi$. The induced map on rational homology commutes with the regular representation of $K$ on the rank-one $K$-space $K$; its commutant is $K$ itself. Hence $u_\varphi=\varphi(u)$ for one $u\in K$, and lattice preservation says $u\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak a$. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal O}(A(\mathfrak a,\Phi))
=(\mathfrak a:\mathfrak a) \tag{5.7}
$$

whenever the specified order $\mathcal O$ spans $K$ over $\mathbf Q$.

### 5.3 Homomorphisms and isogenies

Let $\mathfrak a,\mathfrak b$ be full lattices for the same type. The preceding commutant argument gives

$$
\operatorname{Hom}_K(A(\mathfrak a,\Phi),A(\mathfrak b,\Phi))
=\{u\in K:u\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak b\}. \tag{5.8}
$$

The map attached to nonzero $u$ is an isogeny, and its kernel is

$$
\mathfrak b/u\mathfrak a
$$

after identifying points through the universal cover. Thus

$$
\deg(u)=[\mathfrak b:u\mathfrak a]. \tag{5.9}
$$

In particular the two tori are $K$-linearly isomorphic precisely when $\mathfrak b=u\mathfrak a$ for some $u\in K^\times$.

If $\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak b$, the identity on $\mathbf C^\Phi$ induces

$$
A(\mathfrak a,\Phi)\longrightarrow A(\mathfrak b,\Phi)
$$

of degree $[\mathfrak b:\mathfrak a]$. The arrow points toward the larger lattice because quotienting by a larger subgroup produces a smaller torus. This elementary direction is responsible for the inverse ideal in the class-group action.

For a CM algebra, homomorphisms respecting all idempotents split componentwise. Without that qualification, isomorphic simple factors can contribute off-diagonal homomorphisms; the field-by-field formula (5.8) is then replaced by a matrix algebra.

### 5.4 The ideal action

Fix a CM order $\mathcal O$ and a proper invertible fractional $\mathcal O$-ideal $\mathfrak a$. For an invertible fractional $\mathcal O$-ideal $\mathfrak b$, define

$$
\mathfrak b*A(\mathfrak a,\Phi)
=A(\mathfrak b^{-1}\mathfrak a,\Phi). \tag{5.10}
$$

If $\mathfrak b$ is integral, then $\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak b^{-1}\mathfrak a$, so the identity on the universal cover gives an isogeny

$$
q_{\mathfrak b}:A(\mathfrak a,\Phi)
\longrightarrow \mathfrak b*A(\mathfrak a,\Phi), \tag{5.11}
$$

whose kernel is

$$
A[\mathfrak b]
=\{x\in A:a x=0\text{ for every }a\in\mathfrak b\}
\simeq\mathfrak b^{-1}\mathfrak a/\mathfrak a. \tag{5.12}
$$

Locally free rank one implies

$$
\deg q_{\mathfrak b}=[\mathfrak b^{-1}\mathfrak a:\mathfrak a]
=|\mathcal O/\mathfrak b|. \tag{5.13}
$$

The inverse in (5.10) makes composition covariant:

$$
\mathfrak c*(\mathfrak b*A)=
(\mathfrak c\mathfrak b)*A. \tag{5.14}
$$

Principal ideals act trivially on isomorphism classes, because multiplication by $u^{-1}$ identifies $A(\mathfrak a)$ with $A(u^{-1}\mathfrak a)$. Hence $\operatorname{Pic}(\mathcal O)$ acts on $K$-linear isomorphism classes with proper invertible lattice and multiplier order $\mathcal O$. On that set the action is simply transitive: (5.8) gives both transitivity and freeness. If the starting lattice is proper but noninvertible, the same transform by invertible ideals is defined, but its orbit need not contain every proper lattice.

At primes dividing the conductor, a noninvertible ideal can still define a quotient, but it does not belong to a group action and its multiplier ring may grow. Statements using the class group will always retain invertibility or a prime-to-conductor hypothesis.

## 6. Riemann forms and CM polarizations

The analytic torus becomes an abelian variety only when its lattice carries an integral alternating form compatible with the complex structure and positive in every complex direction. In the CM setting all three conditions reduce to exact statements about one purely imaginary element $\xi$ and one ideal product $\bar{\mathfrak a}\mathfrak a$.

### 6.1 Alternating trace forms

Let $\xi\in K$ satisfy $\bar\xi=-\xi$. Define $E_\xi$ by (1.1):

$$
E_\xi(x,y)=\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbf Q}(\xi\bar x y).
$$

It is $\mathbf Q$-bilinear. Moreover

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_\xi(y,x)
&=\operatorname{Tr}(\xi\bar yx)\\
&=\operatorname{Tr}(\overline{\xi\bar yx})\\
&=\operatorname{Tr}(-\xi y\bar x)
=-E_\xi(x,y),
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1}
$$

so it is alternating. It is nondegenerate when $\xi\ne0$, because the trace pairing is nondegenerate and conjugation and multiplication by $\xi$ are automorphisms of the underlying rational vector space.

The adjoint of multiplication by $a\in K$ is multiplication by $\bar a$:

$$
E_\xi(ax,y)=E_\xi(x,\bar a y). \tag{6.2}
$$

This identity anticipates the Rosati involution. It also shows why a polarization compatible with CM cannot have an arbitrary involution on $K$.

Conversely, every rational alternating form $E$ on the rank-one $K$-space $K$ satisfying

$$
E(ax,y)=E(x,\bar a y) \tag{6.3}
$$

has the form $E_\xi$ for a unique $\xi\in K^-$.

**Proof.** By trace duality, the functional $y\mapsto E(1,y)$ is $\operatorname{Tr}(\xi y)$ for a unique $\xi\in K$. Then (6.3) gives

$$
E(x,y)=E(1,\bar x y)=\operatorname{Tr}(\xi\bar x y).
$$

Alternation implies $\operatorname{Tr}((\xi+\bar\xi)\bar x y)=0$ for all $x,y$, hence $\xi+\bar\xi=0$. $\square$

### 6.2 The positivity calculation

Let $J$ be multiplication by $i$ on $\mathbf C^\Phi$. The Riemann relations require $E(Jx,Jy)=E(x,y)$ and the real symmetric form

$$
B(x,y)=E(Jx,y)
$$

to be positive definite.

Write $x=(x_\varphi)_{\varphi\in\Phi}$ under (5.2). Pairing conjugate embeddings in the trace gives

$$
E_\xi(x,y)
=2\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}
\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\varphi(\xi)\overline{x_\varphi}y_\varphi\bigr). \tag{6.4}
$$

Since $\varphi(\xi)$ is purely imaginary, this form is alternating on the underlying real vector space and is $J$-invariant. On the diagonal,

$$
E_\xi(Jx,x)
=2\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi(\xi))|x_\varphi|^2. \tag{6.5}
$$

Therefore:

**Theorem 6.1 (CM Riemann criterion).** The trace form $E_\xi$ is a positive Riemann form for the type $\Phi$ if and only if

$$
\bar\xi=-\xi,
\qquad
\operatorname{Im}\varphi(\xi)>0
\quad(\varphi\in\Phi). \tag{6.6}
$$

We record why “Riemann form” really implies algebraicity here, rather than importing an unproved bridge from complex tori to abelian varieties.

**Theorem 6.2 (Riemann bilinear theorem in the required form).** Let $V$ be a complex vector space, let $\Lambda\subset V$ be a full lattice, and let $J$ denote multiplication by $i$ on the underlying real space. Suppose an integral alternating form $E:\Lambda\times\Lambda\to\mathbf Z$ extends to $V_{\mathbf R}$ and satisfies

$$
E(Jx,Jy)=E(x,y),\qquad E(Jx,x)>0\quad(x\ne0). \tag{6.7}
$$

Then $V/\Lambda$ carries an ample holomorphic line bundle with first Chern form $E$ and is a projective complex torus. Conversely, the first Chern form of an ample line bundle satisfies (6.7).

**Proof strategy.** The alternating form and the complex structure combine into a positive Hermitian form. Integrality supplies a factor of automorphy, and the resulting theta series give enough sections of a high tensor power to embed the torus in projective space.

**Proof.** Put

$$
H(x,y)=E(Jx,y)+iE(x,y).
$$

The first relation in (6.7) makes $E(Jx,y)$ symmetric, so $H$ is Hermitian, and the second makes it positive definite. Its imaginary part is $E$. Choose a symplectic elementary-divisor basis $e_i,f_i$ of $\Lambda$, so $E(e_i,f_j)=d_i\delta_{ij}$ and the pairings among the $e_i$ or among the $f_i$ vanish. For

$$
\lambda=\sum_i(m_i e_i+n_i f_i),
$$

set $\alpha(\lambda)=(-1)^{\sum_i d_i m_i n_i}$. Expanding the exponent shows that this semicharacter satisfies

$$
\alpha(\lambda+\mu)
=\alpha(\lambda)\alpha(\mu)
\exp\bigl(\pi iE(\lambda,\mu)\bigr). \tag{6.8}
$$

The functions

$$
j_\lambda(z)=\alpha(\lambda)
\exp\!\left(\pi H(z,\lambda)+\frac\pi2H(\lambda,\lambda)\right)
$$

satisfy $j_{\lambda+\mu}(z)=j_\lambda(z+\mu)j_\mu(z)$; this is a direct substitution using (6.8) and $\operatorname{Im}H=E$. Hence the action

$$
\lambda\cdot(z,t)=(z+\lambda,j_\lambda(z)t)
$$

on $V\times\mathbf C$ defines a holomorphic line bundle $L(H,\alpha)$ on $V/\Lambda$. Differentiating the factor of automorphy shows that its Chern form is represented by $E$ and that its Hermitian curvature is positive.

For completeness, positivity also supplies projective coordinates. After choosing complementary maximal isotropic sublattices in the elementary-divisor basis, the usual theta sums are indexed by the finite residue classes of one sublattice modulo a sufficiently large multiple. Positive definiteness of $H$ makes these Gaussian series converge normally, so they are holomorphic sections of $L(H,\alpha)^{\otimes m}$. Fourier orthogonality in the isotropic coordinates shows, for all sufficiently large $m$, that the sections have no common zero, separate two distinct points, and separate tangent vectors at every point. Their evaluation map is therefore a holomorphic closed immersion

$$
V/\Lambda\hookrightarrow\mathbf P^N.
$$

The image is a closed analytic subset of projective space and hence algebraic; the pulled-back hyperplane bundle is a positive power of $L(H,\alpha)$. Thus the torus is projective and the constructed line bundle is ample. Conversely, the Chern form of an ample line bundle is integral and of type $(1,1)$, giving $J$-invariance, while positivity of its curvature gives $E(Jx,x)>0$. $\square$

Equation (6.5) is the decisive sign check. Had we defined $E$ as $\operatorname{Tr}(\xi x\bar y)$, the same calculation would demand negative imaginary parts. Many apparent disagreements about CM polarizations are nothing more than this switch of variables.

### 6.3 Integrality, dual lattices, and degree

For an arbitrary full lattice $\mathfrak a$, the form is integral exactly when

$$
\xi\bar{\mathfrak a}\subseteq\mathfrak a^t. \tag{6.9}
$$

Suppose more particularly that $\mathfrak a$ is a proper invertible ideal of a conjugation-stable order $\mathcal O$. Because $\mathfrak a\mathcal O=\mathfrak a$, condition (6.9) is equivalent to

$$
\xi\bar{\mathfrak a}\mathfrak a
\subseteq\mathcal O^t. \tag{6.10}
$$

For $\mathcal O=\mathcal O_K$, this becomes $\xi\bar{\mathfrak a}\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak D_K^{-1}$. The order trace dual is essential at conductor primes; replacing it by the inverse different of the maximal order would impose the wrong integral condition.

Define the $E_\xi$-dual lattice

$$
\mathfrak a^{\#,\xi}
=\{x\in K:E_\xi(x,\mathfrak a)\subseteq\mathbf Z\}. \tag{6.11}
$$

For a proper invertible $\mathcal O$-ideal, trace duality gives

$$
\mathfrak a^{\#,\xi}
=\xi^{-1}\mathcal O^t\bar{\mathfrak a}^{-1}. \tag{6.12}
$$

The harmless sign arising from $\bar\xi=-\xi$ does not change the lattice. Indeed, the condition is $\xi\bar x\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathcal O^t$; conjugating and solving for $x$ gives (6.12). For the maximal order, $\mathcal O^t=\mathfrak D_K^{-1}$.

When (6.9) holds, $\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak a^{\#,\xi}$. Under analytic uniformization, the polarization homomorphism has kernel canonically isomorphic to the finite quotient

$$
\mathfrak a^{\#,\xi}/\mathfrak a,
$$

and its degree is

$$
\deg\lambda_\xi
=[\mathfrak a^{\#,\xi}:\mathfrak a]. \tag{6.13}
$$

This is the determinant of an integral alternating matrix. Its order is a square, in agreement with the general theory of polarizations. If its elementary divisors are $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_g$, then the polarization type is $(d_1,\ldots,d_g)$ and the index equals $(d_1\cdots d_g)^2$.

### 6.4 Principal polarizations

The polarization is principal exactly when the lattice is self-dual:

$$
\mathfrak a=\mathfrak a^{\#,\xi}.
$$

For a proper invertible $\mathcal O$-ideal, (6.12) turns this into the ideal equation

$$
\xi\mathfrak a\bar{\mathfrak a}
=\mathcal O^t. \tag{6.14}
$$

Together with $\bar\xi=-\xi$ and positivity (6.6), equation (6.14) is the complete principal-polarization criterion. For $\mathcal O_K$ it reads $\xi\mathfrak a\bar{\mathfrak a}=\mathfrak D_K^{-1}$.

Not every ideal class satisfies it. In the maximal-order case, taking ideal classes in (6.14) requires

$$
[\mathfrak a\bar{\mathfrak a}]
=[\mathfrak D_K^{-1}]
$$

after allowing the principal factor $(\xi)$. Even when the class obstruction vanishes, a generator must be purely imaginary and have the required signs. Multiplication by a unit $u\in\mathcal O_K^\times$ changes the generator; preserving pure imaginarity forces $u/\bar u$ to interact correctly, and preserving positivity imposes a totally positive condition on the real factor.

Every CM torus is nevertheless polarizable. Choose positive imaginary $\xi_0$ and multiply it by a sufficiently divisible positive integer so that $m\xi_0\bar{\mathfrak a}\subseteq\mathfrak a^t$. This proves projectivity but generally produces a nonprincipal polarization.

For an imaginary quadratic field, $\mathfrak D_K^{-1}$ is generated by a purely imaginary element up to a rational positive factor, and every ideal product $\mathfrak a\bar{\mathfrak a}$ is generated by the positive rational norm of $\mathfrak a$. Thus every CM elliptic curve has its canonical principal polarization. In higher dimension the ideal-class and unit-sign obstructions become genuine.

### 6.5 Rosati involutions and CM conjugation

Let $\lambda_\xi$ be the polarization from $E_\xi$. The associated Rosati involution on $\operatorname{End}^0(A)$ is

$$
u\longmapsto u^\dagger=\lambda_\xi^{-1}u^\vee\lambda_\xi.
$$

Equation (6.2) proves, on the embedded field $K$, that

$$
a^\dagger=\bar a. \tag{6.15}
$$

Indeed, duality with respect to the alternating form is characterized by the adjoint identity, and (6.2) identifies that adjoint. This also proves positivity:

$$
\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbf Q}(a a^\dagger)
=\sum_{\varphi\in\Sigma_K}|\varphi(a)|^2>0
$$

for $a\ne0$.

Conversely, suppose a polarization on a CM abelian variety has Rosati involution preserving $K$. A positive involution on the CM field must be its complex conjugation by Proposition 2.1. Hence (6.15) is forced, not an extra normalization. This is the precise polarization hypothesis used in CM and PEL moduli: the action must intertwine the specified involution on the coefficient algebra with Rosati.

## 7. Classification over the complex numbers

The analytic construction would be incomplete if it produced only examples. In fact every complex abelian variety with full CM by a field arises from a type and a lattice, and every compatible polarization arises from one of the trace forms above. This chapter proves those converses.

### 7.1 From a CM abelian variety to a rank-one module

Let $A/\mathbf C$ have dimension $g$, and suppose there is an embedding

$$
\iota:K\hookrightarrow\operatorname{End}^0(A)
$$

with $[K:\mathbf Q]=2g$. Then $V=H_1(A,\mathbf Q)$ has dimension $2g$ over $\mathbf Q$ and is a faithful $K$-module. Since $K$ is a field,

$$
\dim_{\mathbf Q}V=[K:\mathbf Q]\dim_KV,
$$

so $\dim_KV=1$. Choose a $K$-linear isomorphism $V\simeq K$. The integral homology lattice $H_1(A,\mathbf Z)$ becomes a full lattice $\mathfrak a\subset K$.

Different choices of $V\simeq K$ multiply $\mathfrak a$ by an element of $K^\times$. Thus the associated ideal class, not the literal lattice, is intrinsic. The order acting integrally is

$$
\iota^{-1}(\operatorname{End}(A))=(\mathfrak a:\mathfrak a). \tag{7.1}
$$

If $K$ is replaced by a commutative semisimple algebra of dimension $2g$, its idempotents split $V$ and $A$ up to isogeny, reducing the analysis to its CM field factors.

### 7.2 Recovering the type and lattice

Because every $a\in K$ is a Hodge endomorphism, the decomposition

$$
V_{\mathbf C}=H^{-1,0}\oplus H^{0,-1}
$$

is stable under $K$. Each embedding eigenspace is one-dimensional. Let $\Phi$ be the embeddings whose eigenspaces occur in $H^{-1,0}$. Complex conjugation exchanges the two Hodge summands, so $\Phi$ contains exactly one member of each conjugate pair.

Under $V\simeq K$, the real complex structure agrees with the one on $\mathbf C^\Phi$ in (5.2). Therefore the analytic uniformization of $A$ becomes

$$
A(\mathbf C)\simeq\mathbf C^\Phi/\Phi(\mathfrak a). \tag{7.2}
$$

This isomorphism respects the $K$-action. We have proved:

**Theorem 7.1 (unpolarized classification).** Labelled complex abelian varieties of dimension $g$ with an action of a CM field $K$ of degree $2g$ are classified by pairs $(\Phi,\mathfrak a)$, where $\Phi$ is a CM type and $\mathfrak a$ is a full lattice in $K$, modulo

$$
(\Phi,\mathfrak a)\sim(\Phi,u\mathfrak a)
\quad(u\in K^\times).
$$

If the labelling by $K$ is forgotten, one must also quotient by transport through automorphisms of $K$.

The theorem is about a chosen full CM field action. An abelian variety can contain several isomorphic copies of a CM field in its rational endomorphism algebra, especially when it is not simple. Forgetting which copy acts loses information that the type alone cannot restore.

### 7.3 The polarized classification

Suppose $(A,\lambda,\iota)$ is polarized and Rosati restricts to conjugation on $K$. Pull the Riemann form of $\lambda$ back to $V\simeq K$. Its adjoint property is (6.3), so Section 6.1 gives a unique $\xi\in K^-$ with

$$
E=E_\xi.
$$

The Riemann positivity condition and lattice integrality give

$$
\operatorname{Im}\varphi(\xi)>0\quad(\varphi\in\Phi),
\qquad
\xi\bar{\mathfrak a}\subseteq\mathfrak a^t. \tag{7.3}
$$

For a proper invertible ideal of a CM order $\mathcal O$, the second condition is equivalently $\xi\bar{\mathfrak a}\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathcal O^t$; only for the maximal order may one replace $\mathcal O^t$ by $\mathfrak D_K^{-1}$. Conversely the conditions in (7.3) construct a polarized CM abelian variety. Hence polarized labelled objects are triples

$$
(\Phi,\mathfrak a,\xi)
$$

satisfying (7.3). Changing the $K$-basis by $u\in K^\times$ replaces

$$
\mathfrak a\longmapsto u\mathfrak a,
\qquad
\xi\longmapsto\frac{\xi}{u\bar u}. \tag{7.4}
$$

Indeed,

$$
E_{\xi/(u\bar u)}(ux,uy)=E_\xi(x,y). \tag{7.5}
$$

For a proper invertible $\mathcal O$-ideal, the principal objects are exactly those satisfying (6.14). Formula (7.4) is indispensable: quotienting lattices by $K^\times$ while holding $\xi$ fixed would identify nonisomorphic polarized objects.

An isogeny given by $u\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak b$ pulls back a polarization $E_\eta$ to $E_{\bar u\eta u}=E_{u\bar u\eta}$. Thus it is a polarized similitude with multiplier $m\in\mathbf Q_{>0}$ exactly when

$$
u\bar u\eta=m\xi. \tag{7.6}
$$

This is the element-level form of the similitude condition later satisfied by reflex norms.

### 7.4 Simplicity and primitive types

If $\Phi$ is induced from $(K_0,\Phi_0)$, then the rational Hodge structure $K$ is a direct sum of $[K:K_0]$ copies of $K_0$ with its CM Hodge structure. Isogeny classes of complex abelian varieties correspond to polarizable rational Hodge structures of type $(-1,0)+(0,-1)$, so

$$
A(K,\Phi)\sim A(K_0,\Phi_0)^{[K:K_0]}. \tag{7.7}
$$

Thus an induced type does not give a simple abelian variety.

We justify both the Hodge-theoretic bridge and the converse. If $W\subset H_1(A,\mathbf Q)$ is a rational Hodge substructure, then $W_\mathbf R/(W\cap H_1(A,\mathbf Z))$ is a closed complex subtorus. The restriction of a polarization is a positive integral Riemann form, so Theorem 6.2 makes this subtorus an abelian subvariety. Conversely, the homology of an abelian subvariety is a rational Hodge substructure. Thus $A$ is simple up to isogeny exactly when its rational Hodge structure is simple.

The remaining linear-algebra statement is the **induction lemma**: the CM Hodge structure on $K$ defined by $\Phi$ is simple if and only if $\Phi$ is not induced from a proper CM subfield. Here is a proof. Let $L$ be a Galois CM field containing the normal closure of $K$, put $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbf Q)$ and $H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$, and identify the embeddings of $K$ with $X=G/H$. Write $\epsilon:X\to\{0,1\}$ for the characteristic function of $\Phi$. After scalar extension to $L$, a rational endomorphism of the underlying vector space is a matrix $(a_{xy})_{x,y\in X}$ satisfying the Galois rule

$$
a_{\gamma x,\gamma y}=\gamma(a_{xy}). \tag{7.8}
$$

It is a Hodge endomorphism exactly when

$$
a_{xy}=0\quad\text{if }\epsilon(x)\ne\epsilon(y), \tag{7.9}
$$

because a Hodge map cannot mix the two bidegrees. These two assertions follow by writing $K\otimes_{\mathbf Q}L=\prod_{x\in X}L$ and descending the coordinate matrix.

Suppose the Hodge structure has a nontrivial direct summand, and let $p$ be the associated idempotent. A diagonal matrix satisfying (7.8) is multiplication by an element of $K$; if it is idempotent, the field property makes it $0$ or $1$. Hence a nontrivial idempotent has an off-diagonal entry. On $X$, take the equivalence relation generated by pairs $(x,y)$ for which $p$ has nonzero $(x,y)$-entry. Equation (7.8) makes the relation $G$-invariant, and (7.9) makes $\epsilon$ constant on every equivalence class. Let $H_0$ be the stabilizer of the class of $H$. Then

$$
H\subsetneq H_0\subsetneq G. \tag{7.10}
$$

The first inclusion is strict because an off-diagonal entry occurs. The second is strict because a class is monochromatic, whereas central complex conjugation interchanges $\Phi$ and $\bar\Phi$. Put $K_0=L^{H_0}$. Central conjugation normalizes $H_0$ but is not in it, so $K_0$ is CM. Constancy of $\epsilon$ on the $H_0$-orbits says precisely

$$
\varphi\in\Phi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\varphi|_{K_0}\in\Phi_0
$$

for one CM type $\Phi_0$ on $K_0$. Hence $\Phi$ is induced. Conversely, if $\epsilon$ factors through $G/H_0$, then choosing a $K_0$-basis of $K$ identifies its Hodge structure with $[K:K_0]$ copies of the one on $K_0$; projection onto one copy is a nontrivial rational Hodge idempotent. This proves the induction lemma without assuming the desired simplicity criterion.

This proves the standard criterion.

**Theorem 7.2.** A CM abelian variety associated with a field type $(K,\Phi)$ is simple up to isogeny if and only if $\Phi$ is primitive.

More precisely, every CM abelian variety is isogenous to a product of powers of simple CM abelian varieties attached to primitive types. “Simple” here means over $\mathbf C$; arithmetic absolute simplicity over a smaller field is a separate descent question.

## 8. Endomorphisms, fields of definition, and determinant data

Complex uniformization identifies all CM endomorphisms explicitly, but arithmetic applications require knowing what survives over a smaller base field. This chapter separates three statements that are often blurred: an analytic homomorphism is algebraic, an algebraic endomorphism is defined over some finite extension, and the full CM action is defined over a specified field only under an actual invariance hypothesis.

### 8.1 Analytic and algebraic homomorphisms

Let $A=V/\Lambda$ and $B=W/M$ be complex abelian varieties. Every holomorphic group homomorphism $f:A\to B$ lifts uniquely to a complex-linear map $F:V\to W$ satisfying $F(\Lambda)\subseteq M$. It is algebraic.

One proof uses the graph. The graph $\Gamma_f$ is a closed complex subtorus of $A\times B$. A product polarization restricts to a positive integral Riemann form on it, so Theorem 6.2 makes $\Gamma_f$ projective. Under projective embeddings, its image is a closed analytic subset of projective space and is algebraic by the same analytic-to-algebraic step used in Theorem 6.2. The two projections of the algebraic graph are algebraic; the first is an isomorphism because it becomes one analytically. Composing its inverse with the second projection proves that $f$ is algebraic.

Consequently the lattice calculation (5.8) computes algebraic homomorphisms, not merely analytic ones. It also proves that every isomorphism and isogeny used in the ideal action belongs to the algebraic category.

### 8.2 Descent of individual endomorphisms

Let $A$ be an abelian variety over a field $k$ of characteristic zero, let $\bar k$ be an algebraic closure, and let $u\in\operatorname{End}(A_{\bar k})$. The graph of $u$ is cut out by finitely many equations with coefficients in $\bar k$, so those coefficients lie in a finite extension $k'/k$. Thus every individual geometric endomorphism is defined over some finite extension.

There is an intrinsic criterion. The absolute Galois group acts on geometric endomorphisms by

$$
{}^\sigma u=\sigma\circ u\circ\sigma^{-1}. \tag{8.1}
$$

**Proposition 8.1.** The endomorphism $u$ is defined over $k$ if and only if ${}^\sigma u=u$ for every $\sigma\in G_k$.

**Proof.** Necessity is immediate. For sufficiency, the invariant graph $\Gamma_u\subset A_{\bar k}\times A_{\bar k}$ descends by Galois descent for closed subschemes. Its first projection descends to an isomorphism because that property may be checked after the faithfully flat extension $\bar k/k$. The second projection then gives the descended endomorphism. $\square$

Since $\operatorname{End}(A_{\bar k})$ is a finitely generated free abelian group, its automorphism group carries the discrete topology. The continuous image of the compact profinite group $G_k$ in this discrete group is compact and discrete, hence finite. The kernel is therefore open and corresponds to one finite extension defining all geometric endomorphisms. This does **not** say the reflex field defines them, nor that the field of moduli is a field of definition. Those stronger conclusions require CM reciprocity and descent data.

If a $K$-action is defined over $k$, then every $\sigma\in G_k$ commutes with it on homology and on the tangent space. If only its conjugacy class is defined, Galois may transport the action through an automorphism of $K$. A moduli problem must specify which version it uses.

### 8.3 The determinant condition

Let $S$ be an $E$-scheme and let an order $\mathcal O\subset K$ act on an abelian scheme $A/S$. Over $\mathbf C$, type $\Phi$ means that $a\in\mathcal O$ acts on $\operatorname{Lie}A$ with eigenvalues $\varphi(a)$ for $\varphi\in\Phi$. Hence

$$
\det(T-\iota(a)\mid\operatorname{Lie}A)
=\prod_{\varphi\in\Phi}(T-\varphi(a)). \tag{8.2}
$$

The right side has coefficients in the reflex field, as Chapter 9 will prove. Therefore it makes sense over an $E$-base. Equation (8.2), required universally after base change, is the **determinant condition** associated with $\Phi$.

It is stronger than matching dimensions. If $K$ is not a field over the residue characteristic or if embeddings coalesce modulo a prime, one cannot recover the condition by listing geometric eigenvalues. The polynomial identity remains meaningful and imposes the correct multiplicities scheme-theoretically. Checking it only on the members of a $\mathbf Z$-basis is not sufficient: two polynomial laws can agree at those finitely many points and differ on their sums. One may either require (8.2) for every $a$, or impose equality of the two polynomial laws on the universal element $\sum_iX_i a_i$ for a chosen basis $(a_i)$. The latter is a finite list of coefficient identities and is manifestly stable under base change.

For a CM algebra $K=\prod K_i$, the idempotents decompose $\operatorname{Lie}A$, and (8.2) is the product of the type polynomials on the factors. The ranks of the idempotent summands are part of the condition.

### 8.4 What polarization descent requires

Suppose $(A,\iota,\lambda)$ is defined over $k$. Compatibility of the polarization with the CM action is the identity

$$
\lambda\circ\iota(\bar a)=\iota(a)^\vee\circ\lambda
\qquad(a\in\mathcal O). \tag{8.3}
$$

After passing to the generic fiber or to $\mathbf C$, this says that Rosati restricts to conjugation. It is a closed equality of homomorphisms and therefore descends whenever the three displayed pieces do.

A polarization homomorphism can descend even when a particular ample line bundle inducing it does not. This distinction is already present for abelian schemes: a line bundle may carry a Picard or Brauer descent obstruction, whereas the symmetric homomorphism to the dual is invariant. Accordingly polarized moduli use $\lambda:A\to A^\vee$ as the datum and only locally represent it by an ample bundle.

Likewise, an isogeny class may descend without a chosen member, and a CM action may descend only after conjugation. Structural CM theory provides criteria for each piece separately; it does not manufacture an effective descent datum. The reciprocity theorem in the next volume will supply a canonical arithmetic comparison in its stated cases.

## 9. The reflex field

The type is a set of complex embeddings, so at first sight it seems to depend on the ambient copy of $\mathbf C$. The reflex field measures exactly how much of that embedding choice is algebraic. It is not generally the CM field $K$, nor its maximal real subfield, nor its Galois closure. It is the field of definition of the type cocharacter and of the characteristic polynomials in the determinant condition.

### 9.1 The Hodge cocharacter

Let

$$
T_K=\operatorname{Res}_{K/\mathbf Q}\mathbf G_m.
$$

Over $\mathbf C$ there is a canonical splitting

$$
(T_K)_{\mathbf C}\simeq
\prod_{\varphi\in\Sigma_K}\mathbf G_{m,\mathbf C}, \tag{9.1}
$$

where $a\in K^\times$ maps to $(\varphi(a))_\varphi$. Define the Hodge cocharacter

$$
\mu_\Phi:\mathbf G_{m,\mathbf C}\longrightarrow(T_K)_{\mathbf C} \tag{9.2}
$$

by

$$
(\mu_\Phi(z))_\varphi=
\begin{cases}
z,&\varphi\in\Phi,\\
1,&\varphi\in\bar\Phi.
\end{cases} \tag{9.3}
$$

This matches our homology convention: it acts with weight one on the tangent coordinates indexed by $\Phi$. Authors using cohomology may write $z^{-1}$ instead. The field fixed by the cocharacter stabilizer is unchanged by simultaneous inversion, but the resulting reciprocity formulas are inverted. Equation (9.3) is our fixed normalization.

On the cocharacter lattice

$$
X_*(T_K)=\mathbf Z[\Sigma_K], \tag{9.4}
$$

the cocharacter is the vector

$$
\mu_\Phi=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}[\varphi]. \tag{9.5}
$$

The Galois action is $\gamma[\varphi]=[\gamma\circ\varphi]$, so

$$
\gamma\mu_\Phi=\mu_{\gamma\Phi}. \tag{9.6}
$$

This equality makes the stabilizer completely explicit.

### 9.2 Definition and elementary characterizations

The **reflex field** of $(K,\Phi)$ is

$$
E=E(K,\Phi)=\overline{\mathbf Q}^{\Gamma_\Phi}, \tag{9.7}
$$

where $\Gamma_\Phi$ is (3.6). Equivalently, $E$ is the field of definition of the cocharacter $\mu_\Phi$.

There is a concrete generator description.

**Theorem 9.1.** The reflex field is generated over $\mathbf Q$ by the type traces

$$
\operatorname{Tr}_\Phi(a)=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\varphi(a)
\qquad(a\in K). \tag{9.8}
$$

It is also generated by the coefficients of

$$
P_{\Phi,a}(T)=\prod_{\varphi\in\Phi}(T-\varphi(a)) \tag{9.9}
$$

as $a$ ranges over $K$.

**Proof strategy.** An automorphism fixes the type if and only if it fixes every symmetric polynomial in the selected embedding values. Type traces already distinguish subsets of embeddings because embeddings are linearly independent as functions on $K$.

**Proof.** If $\gamma\Phi=\Phi$, then $\gamma$ permutes the summands in (9.8) and the factors in (9.9), so it fixes every displayed value.

Conversely, suppose $\gamma$ fixes $\operatorname{Tr}_\Phi(a)$ for every $a\in K$. Then

$$
\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\varphi(a)
=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}(\gamma\varphi)(a)
$$

for all $a$. Distinct field embeddings are linearly independent over $\mathbf C$: if $\sum c_i\varphi_i=0$, choose an element separating one embedding from the others and use the usual minimal-support argument after multiplying the relation by that element. Therefore the two multisets of embeddings agree, so $\gamma\Phi=\Phi$. This proves the trace assertion.

The coefficients in (9.9) include the type trace up to sign. Conversely, Newton identities express all coefficients using the power sums $\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\varphi(a)^n=\operatorname{Tr}_\Phi(a^n)$. Hence the two generated fields agree. $\square$

Taking the constant coefficient in (9.9) shows that the type norm belongs to $E$:

$$
N_\Phi:K^\times\longrightarrow E^\times,
\qquad a\longmapsto\prod_{\varphi\in\Phi}\varphi(a). \tag{9.10}
$$

It points from $K$ to $E$ and is not the reflex norm.

### 9.3 A Galois-closure computation

Let $L/\mathbf Q$ be a finite Galois extension containing $K$, put

$$
G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbf Q),
\qquad H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K),
$$

and identify $\Sigma_K$ with left cosets $G/H$ by $sH\mapsto s|_K$. Let $S\subset G/H$ correspond to $\Phi$, and let $\widetilde S\subset G$ be its inverse image. Thus $\widetilde S$ is a union of left cosets $sH$. The stabilizer of the type is

$$
H^*=\{\gamma\in G:\gamma\widetilde S=\widetilde S\}, \tag{9.11}
$$

and

$$
E=L^{H^*}. \tag{9.12}
$$

This is independent of the chosen Galois closure because it computes the fixed field of the absolute stabilizer.

For $a\in K$, the product

$$
\prod_{sH\in S}s(a) \tag{9.13}
$$

is fixed by $H^*$ and therefore lies in $E$. Formula (9.11) is computationally useful: orbit--stabilizer gives $[E:\mathbf Q]=|G:H^*|$, the number of distinct Galois conjugates of the type.

If $K/\mathbf Q$ is Galois, then $H=1$ and a type is literally a subset $S\subset G$. The reflex field is the fixed field of the left stabilizer of $S$. For $K=\mathbf Q(\zeta_5)$ and $S=\{1,2\}$ in $(\mathbf Z/5\mathbf Z)^\times$, the stabilizer is trivial, so $E=K$. The reflex type will correspond to $S^{-1}=\{1,3\}$; confusing $S$ and $S^{-1}$ is the elementary source of the variance addressed in Chapter 10.

### 9.4 Fields of definition of trace and determinant data

Theorem 9.1 shows that every polynomial $P_{\Phi,a}(T)$ lies in $E[T]$. It also proves minimality: if all these polynomials have coefficients in a subfield $E_0\subset\mathbf C$, then every automorphism fixing $E_0$ fixes all type traces, hence stabilizes $\Phi$, so it fixes $E$. Therefore $E\subseteq E_0$.

Thus the determinant condition (8.2) is naturally defined over $E$, and no smaller field works for the full labelled type data. A particular abelian variety can have accidental descent to a smaller field, especially after forgetting its CM labelling or polarization. That does not shrink the reflex field: the reflex field belongs to the pair $(K,\Phi)$, not to one unlabelled point.

The reflex field is itself CM. To see this, choose a finite Galois CM extension $L$ containing the normal closure of $K$; such an $L$ is obtained by adjoining the conjugates of $K$, and its complex conjugation $c$ is central. The subgroup $H^*$ of (9.11) is normalized by $c$, while $c\notin H^*$ because $c\Phi=\bar\Phi\ne\Phi$. Thus $c$ induces a nonidentity involution on $E=L^{H^*}$, its fixed field lies in the totally real field $L^{\langle c\rangle}$, and $E$ is quadratic and totally imaginary over that fixed field. The complex conjugate type has the conjugate embedded reflex field $\bar E$. For a primitive type, $E$ carries an ordinary reflex type; for an imprimitive type it is safer to work directly with the cocharacter and reflex norm, because naive inverse-set formulas can obscure induced multiplicities.

## 10. The reflex norm

The type norm multiplied the selected embeddings of an element of $K$ and landed in $E$. The reflex norm must travel in the opposite direction. Its intrinsic definition is a morphism of rational tori characterized by one cocharacter. From that definition the Galois-closure product, conjugation identity, ideal map, and polarization multiplier all follow without guesses about inverses.

### 10.1 Restriction-of-scalars tori and their lattices

For a number field $F$, put $T_F=\operatorname{Res}_{F/\mathbf Q}\mathbf G_m$. Over $\overline{\mathbf Q}$,

$$
(T_F)_{\overline{\mathbf Q}}
\simeq\prod_{\tau\in\Sigma_F}\mathbf G_m.
$$

Its character and cocharacter lattices are permutation modules

$$
X^*(T_F)=\mathbf Z[\Sigma_F]^\vee,
\qquad
X_*(T_F)=\mathbf Z[\Sigma_F]. \tag{10.1}
$$

A morphism $f:T_E\to T_K$ is determined contravariantly by

$$
f^*:X^*(T_K)\longrightarrow X^*(T_E), \tag{10.2}
$$

or covariantly by

$$
f_*:X_*(T_E)\longrightarrow X_*(T_K). \tag{10.3}
$$

Both maps must be Galois equivariant. Confusing (10.2) with (10.3) reverses the field direction; this is why we define the norm using (10.3) and then derive the element formula.

Choose the distinguished embedding $\iota_E:E\hookrightarrow\mathbf C$. Let $[\iota_E]$ be its basis cocharacter. Its stabilizer is $G_E$, which fixes $\mu_\Phi$ by definition.

### 10.2 Definition by cocharacters

There is a unique Galois-equivariant homomorphism

$$
(r_\Phi)_*:X_*(T_E)\longrightarrow X_*(T_K) \tag{10.4}
$$

such that

$$
(r_\Phi)_*[\iota_E]=\mu_\Phi. \tag{10.5}
$$

Every embedding of $E$ has the form $\gamma\iota_E$, and equivariance forces

$$
(r_\Phi)_*[\gamma\iota_E]=\gamma\mu_\Phi. \tag{10.6}
$$

This is well defined precisely because two choices of $\gamma$ differ by an element fixing $E$, hence fixing $\mu_\Phi$.

**Definition 10.1.** The **reflex norm** is the rational torus morphism

$$
r_\Phi:T_E\longrightarrow T_K \tag{10.7}
$$

whose cocharacter map is (10.4)--(10.6). On rational points it is

$$
r_\Phi:E^\times\longrightarrow K^\times. \tag{10.8}
$$

For a character basis element $e_\varphi\in X^*(T_K)$, duality gives

$$
r_\Phi^*(e_\varphi)
=\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma_E}
\langle e_\varphi,\gamma_\tau\mu_\Phi\rangle e_\tau, \tag{10.9}
$$

where $\gamma_\tau|_E=\tau$. Each coefficient is $0$ or $1$: it is $1$ precisely when $\gamma_\tau^{-1}\varphi\in\Phi$. Formula (10.9) is independent of the chosen lifts.

### 10.3 The product formula in a Galois closure

Retain $L$, $G$, $H$, $S$, and $H^*$ from Section 9.3. Embeddings of $E$ correspond to $G/H^*$. Define the inverse incidence relation

$$
m(\tau,\varphi)=
\begin{cases}
1,&\gamma_\tau^{-1}\varphi\in\Phi,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases} \tag{10.10}
$$

Then for $b\in E^\times$, the $\varphi$-coordinate of $r_\Phi(b)$ in $L$ is

$$
\varphi(r_\Phi(b))
=\prod_{\tau\in\Sigma_E}\tau(b)^{m(\tau,\varphi)}. \tag{10.11}
$$

The right side lies in $\varphi(K)$ because (10.9) comes from a rational torus morphism.

When $(K,\Phi)$ is primitive, the inverse incidence is an ordinary CM type $\Phi^*$ on $E$. In Galois-closure notation its embeddings are represented by

$$
S^*=\{s^{-1}H^*:sH\in S\}, \tag{10.12}
$$

with repetitions removed by the stabilizer. Then

$$
r_\Phi(b)=N_{\Phi^*}(b)
=\prod_{\psi\in\Phi^*}\psi(b), \tag{10.13}
$$

interpreted in $K$ through the chosen embeddings in $L$. The inverses in (10.12) are essential.

**Proof of (10.11).** Over $L$, a torus morphism is a matrix of integer exponents. The exponent of the $\tau$-coordinate of $b$ in the $\varphi$-coordinate of the output is

$$
\langle e_\varphi,(r_\Phi)_*[\tau]\rangle
=\langle e_\varphi,\gamma_\tau\mu_\Phi\rangle,
$$

which equals one exactly when $\gamma_\tau^{-1}\varphi\in\Phi$. Multiplying coordinates gives (10.11). $\square$

For $K=\mathbf Q(\zeta_5)$ with $\Phi=\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2\}$, we found $E=K$ and $\Phi^*=\{\sigma_1,\sigma_3\}$. Hence

$$
r_\Phi(b)=\sigma_1(b)\sigma_3(b), \tag{10.14}
$$

after identifying the product with an element of $K$. Using $\{1,2\}$ instead would define the type norm, not the reflex norm.

### 10.4 Conjugation and the weight identity

Let $w_K:\mathbf G_m\to T_K$ be the diagonal cocharacter

$$
w_K=\sum_{\varphi\in\Sigma_K}[\varphi]. \tag{10.15}
$$

Since $\Phi$ contains one embedding from each pair,

$$
\mu_\Phi+c\mu_\Phi=w_K. \tag{10.16}
$$

In a finite Galois CM extension containing the normal closures of $K$ and $E$, complex conjugation is central. Hence for every Galois conjugate, $\gamma\mu_\Phi+c\gamma\mu_\Phi=\gamma(\mu_\Phi+c\mu_\Phi)=w_K$. On every basis cocharacter $[\tau]$ of $T_E$, the product of $r_\Phi$ with its conjugate therefore has cocharacter $w_K$. The field norm $N_{E/\mathbf Q}:T_E\to\mathbf G_m$ followed by the diagonal inclusion $\mathbf G_m\to T_K$ has the same cocharacter map. Therefore:

**Theorem 10.2 (weight identity).** For every $b\in E^\times$,

$$
r_\Phi(b)\,\overline{r_\Phi(b)}
=N_{E/\mathbf Q}(b)\in\mathbf Q^\times. \tag{10.17}
$$

The same identity holds over every $\mathbf Q$-algebra and on finite ideles. It is the structural reason the reflex norm acts by polarization similitudes.

### 10.5 Functoriality and induced types

If $\gamma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbf C/\mathbf Q)$ acts on types by postcomposition as in (3.5), then

$$
E(K,\gamma\Phi)=\gamma E(K,\Phi).
$$

The canonical isomorphism $E\xrightarrow{\gamma}\gamma E$ intertwines the transported cocharacter construction with $r_{\gamma\Phi}$. More generally, every isomorphism of CM pairs transports reflex fields and reflex norms functorially. This is an isomorphism statement, not an assertion that either embedded field is fixed pointwise.

Suppose $K_0\subset K$ and $\Phi$ is induced from $\Phi_0$. Their reflex fields agree in the chosen closure. On cocharacters one checks

$$
N_{K/K_0}\circ r_\Phi
=r_{\Phi_0}^{[K:K_0]} \tag{10.18}
$$

in multiplicative notation: both sides send $[\iota_E]$ to $[K:K_0]\mu_{\Phi_0}$.

For primitive $(K,\Phi)$, the reflex pair $(E,\Phi^*)$ is again primitive, and taking the reflex a second time returns a pair isomorphic to $(K,\Phi)$. Here is the subgroup check hidden in that statement. The lifted type set $\widetilde S\subset G$ is stable on the right by $H$. Its right stabilizer is larger than $H$ exactly when $\widetilde S$ is a union of right cosets of a larger subgroup $H_0$; that is exactly the assertion that membership in $\Phi$ depends only on restriction to the proper CM subfield $L^{H_0}$. Thus primitivity says that the right stabilizer is $H$. The lifted inverse set $\widetilde S^{-1}$ has left stabilizer equal to that right stabilizer, so the reflex field of the reflex type is $L^H=K$. Applying the same argument once more proves primitivity of $\Phi^*$, and $(S^{-1})^{-1}=S$ recovers the original type. This does not say that the composite of the two reflex norm maps is the identity: its exponent matrix is a convolution and generally has higher degree. Double reflexivity concerns the pair and its cocharacter orbit, not cancellation of norm products.

For a CM algebra $K=\prod_iK_i$ with type $\Phi=\coprod_i\Phi_i$, the reflex field is the compositum $E$ of the fields $E_i=E(K_i,\Phi_i)$. The algebra reflex norm is the product of the component maps

$$
T_E\xrightarrow{N_{E/E_i}}T_{E_i}
\xrightarrow{r_{\Phi_i}}T_{K_i}. \tag{10.19}
$$

On the distinguished cocharacter, the $i$th component is $\mu_{\Phi_i}$, so (10.19) is exactly the cocharacter definition for the product type. Each component has the same scalar weight $N_{E/\mathbf Q}$; consequently the image lies in the common-similitude torus required by polarized product PEL data.

## 11. Reflex ideals, finite ideles, and polarized ideal actions

The torus morphism extends simultaneously to every completion. Its finite adelic points act on CM lattices, while its integral exponents turn prime ideals of the reflex field into ideals of the CM field. The weight identity supplies the exact polarization multiplier.

### 11.1 From torus points to finite ideles

For every $\mathbf Q$-algebra $R$, the reflex norm gives

$$
r_\Phi:(E\otimes_{\mathbf Q}R)^\times
\longrightarrow(K\otimes_{\mathbf Q}R)^\times. \tag{11.1}
$$

Taking $R=\mathbf A_f$ yields

$$
r_\Phi:\mathbf A_{E,f}^\times
\longrightarrow\mathbf A_{K,f}^\times. \tag{11.2}
$$

It is continuous and respects principal elements. Locally it is a monomial in components over places linked inside a common normal closure. It is not generally a product of local field norms $E_w\to K_v$, because $E$ and $K$ need not contain one another. The torus formula replaces that nonexistent extension norm.

Let $\widehat{\mathcal O}_F=\prod_p\mathcal O_F\otimes\mathbf Z_p$. For maximal orders, the map sends

$$
\widehat{\mathcal O}_E^\times
\quad\text{into}\quad
\widehat{\mathcal O}_K^\times. \tag{11.3}
$$

Indeed, locally $\mathcal O_{E,p}^\times$ is the maximal compact subgroup of $(E\otimes\mathbf Q_p)^\times$; its image is compact, and every compact subgroup of $(K\otimes\mathbf Q_p)^\times$ has valuation zero in every field factor. For nonmaximal chosen orders, the analogous order-unit assertion is automatic away from conductor primes and requires a direct integral calculation at the conductors. The weight identity becomes

$$
r_\Phi(s)\overline{r_\Phi(s)}
=N_{E/\mathbf Q}(s) \tag{11.4}
$$

in $\mathbf A_{K,f}^\times$.

### 11.2 The reflex ideal map

Let $I_F$ denote the group of nonzero fractional ideals of $\mathcal O_F$. The valuation map gives

$$
\mathbf A_{F,f}^\times/\widehat{\mathcal O}_F^\times
\simeq I_F. \tag{11.5}
$$

The exponent matrix of (10.9) makes (11.2) descend to an ideal homomorphism

$$
R_\Phi:I_E\longrightarrow I_K, \tag{11.6}
$$

called the **reflex ideal norm**. Equivalently, choose a prime in a common Galois closure above a prime of $E$ and distribute its conjugates using (10.10). Galois equivariance makes the result independent of the choice.

For a principal ideal $(b)$,

$$
R_\Phi((b))=(r_\Phi(b)). \tag{11.7}
$$

This equality is a crucial variance check. It gives a map on ideal class groups for maximal orders. For nonmaximal orders, restrict to ideals prime to the conductors so that reflex ideals remain invertible.

Applying valuations to (10.17) gives

$$
R_\Phi(\mathfrak b)\,
\overline{R_\Phi(\mathfrak b)}
=N_{E/\mathbf Q}(\mathfrak b)\mathcal O_K, \tag{11.8}
$$

where the ideal norm on the right is a positive fractional ideal of $\mathbf Q$ extended to $K$. Both sides have equal prime exponents because (10.17) holds for a finite idele representing $\mathfrak b$. The norm is generated by a positive rational number, and by a positive integer when $\mathfrak b$ is integral.

### 11.3 Compatibility with polarizations

Work first with maximal orders. Let $\mathfrak a$ be an invertible $\mathcal O_K$-ideal and let $A=A(\mathfrak a,\Phi)$ be polarized by $E_\xi$. For an invertible $K$-ideal $\mathfrak c$, the ideal action gives

$$
\mathfrak c*A=A(\mathfrak c^{-1}\mathfrak a,\Phi). \tag{11.9}
$$

Integrality of $E_{\xi'}$ on the target requires

$$
\xi'\bar{\mathfrak c}^{-1}\mathfrak c^{-1}
\bar{\mathfrak a}\mathfrak a
\subseteq\mathfrak D_K^{-1}. \tag{11.10}
$$

Suppose $\mathfrak b$ is integral and put $\mathfrak c=R_\Phi(\mathfrak b)$, which is then integral. Equation (11.8) says

$$
\mathfrak c\bar{\mathfrak c}
=n\mathcal O_K,
\qquad n=N_{E/\mathbf Q}(\mathfrak b)>0. \tag{11.11}
$$

Taking $\xi'=n\xi$ makes (11.10) identical to the original integrality condition. If the original polarization is principal, then

$$
n\xi(\mathfrak c^{-1}\mathfrak a)
\overline{(\mathfrak c^{-1}\mathfrak a)}
=\xi\mathfrak a\bar{\mathfrak a}
=\mathfrak D_K^{-1}, \tag{11.12}
$$

so the new polarization is principal. The isogeny $q_\mathfrak c:A\to\mathfrak c*A$ satisfies

$$
q_\mathfrak c^*\lambda_{n\xi}=n\lambda_\xi. \tag{11.13}
$$

The degrees agree. Equation (11.8) implies

$$
N_{K/\mathbf Q}(\mathfrak c)=n^g. \tag{11.14}
$$

Taking degrees in (11.13) gives $(n^g)^2=n^{2g}$, the degree of multiplication by $n$ on a principally polarized $g$-fold. This check detects both a missing conjugate and a reversed ideal action.

For a conjugation-stable nonmaximal order, the same argument is valid for ideals prime to the relevant conductors, with $\mathcal O^t$ in place of $\mathfrak D_K^{-1}$. At conductor primes, invertibility and preservation of the order must be checked before forming the polarized transform.

For a finite idele $s$, choose a fractional ideal represented by it. Changing $s$ by $b\in E^\times$ changes $r_\Phi(s)$ by a principal element and yields an isomorphic torus. Changing it by an integral unit preserves the lattice class away from excluded primes. Thus the finite idele class controls a polarized ideal action up to the expected equivalence.

### 11.4 Level structures and prime-to-level control

Let $N\ge1$ be prime to the discriminants and conductors in use. A full $\mathcal O$-linear level-$N$ structure identifies $A[N]$ with the standard rank-one $\mathcal O/N\mathcal O$-module, with symplectic normalization when a polarization is included. In lattice terms,

$$
A[N](\mathbf C)=N^{-1}\mathfrak a/\mathfrak a. \tag{11.15}
$$

If $\mathfrak c$ is prime to $N$, a local generator at primes dividing $N$ identifies the torsion of $A(\mathfrak a)$ and $A(\mathfrak c^{-1}\mathfrak a)$. This depends on the generator modulo $N$, so an ideal class alone does not act on a chosen full level structure. A finite idele representative records the missing generators.

If $s\in\mathbf A_{E,f}^\times$ is a unit at primes above $N$, then $r_\Phi(s)$ has an image in

$$
(\mathcal O/N\mathcal O)^\times. \tag{11.16}
$$

Under a chosen trivialization of the standard $\mathcal O/N\mathcal O$-module, multiplication by that image is a similitude of the standard CM pairing:

$$
e_N(r_\Phi(s)x,r_\Phi(s)y)
=e_N(x,y)^{N_{E/\mathbf Q}(s)}, \tag{11.17}
$$

where the components of $N_{E/\mathbf Q}(s)$ at primes dividing $N$ are units and therefore define an element of $(\mathbf Z/N\mathbf Z)^\times$. This formula is the reduction of the adjoint identity $a^\dagger=\bar a$ together with $r_\Phi(s)\overline{r_\Phi(s)}=N_{E/\mathbf Q}(s)$; it is not a claim that an ideal class alone canonically identifies two chosen level structures. A symplectic level requires multiplier $1$ modulo $N$; a similitude level records the multiplier. At bad primes one must use the finite group scheme and the exact order action rather than geometric points.

## 12. Mumford--Tate tori and the limits of structural CM theory

The reflex norm is more than an ideal formula. Its image is the rational torus generated by the Hodge cocharacter, so it measures exactly which multiplicative symmetries are visible to the CM Hodge structure. This final chapter identifies that torus, gives a character test for its size, and marks the boundary before reciprocity.

### 12.1 The torus generated by the Hodge cocharacter

Let $M_\Phi\subseteq T_K$ be the smallest $\mathbf Q$-subtorus whose complex cocharacter lattice contains $\mu_\Phi$. Equivalently, its cocharacter lattice is the saturated lattice generated by

$$
\{\gamma\mu_\Phi:\gamma\in G_{\mathbf Q}\}. \tag{12.1}
$$

For the Hodge structure on $K$, this is the Mumford--Tate torus.

**Theorem 12.1.** The image of $r_\Phi:T_E\to T_K$ is $M_\Phi$.

**Proof.** The image torus has cocharacter lattice equal to the saturation of the image of $(r_\Phi)_*$. By (10.6), that image is generated by the vectors $\gamma\mu_\Phi$ as $\gamma$ runs through representatives of embeddings of $E$. These are exactly the distinct Galois conjugates of $\mu_\Phi$. Their saturated span defines $M_\Phi$. $\square$

Thus the reflex norm is the universal torus morphism whose image contains the Hodge cocharacter. Its kernel records multiplicative directions in $E$ invisible to the CM Hodge structure. Surjectivity onto all of $T_K$ is exceptional.

The weight cocharacter belongs to $M_\Phi$ by (10.16). Equation (10.17) says that $r_\Phi$ lands in the unitary similitude torus

$$
\{a\in T_K:a\bar a\in\mathbf G_m\}. \tag{12.2}
$$

This is the torus naturally attached to polarized CM data. Its rational multiplier is $a\bar a$; the unitary subgroup is its multiplier-one kernel.

### 12.2 Character-lattice tests and degeneracy

A character $\chi=\sum_\varphi n_\varphi e_\varphi\in X^*(T_K)$ is trivial on $M_\Phi$ exactly when

$$
\sum_{\varphi\in\gamma\Phi}n_\varphi=0
\qquad\text{for every }\gamma\in G_{\mathbf Q}. \tag{12.3}
$$

Therefore

$$
X^*(M_\Phi)
=X^*(T_K)/
\{\chi:\chi\text{ satisfies }(12.3)\}, \tag{12.4}
$$

after saturating the relation lattice.

This gives an effective rank calculation. Form a matrix whose rows are characteristic vectors of Galois conjugates of $\Phi$ and whose columns are embeddings of $K$. Its rank over $\mathbf Q$ is $\dim M_\Phi$. The relation

$$
1_\Phi+1_{\bar\Phi}=(1,\ldots,1)
$$

always places the rational weight direction in the row span. Further relations reflect degeneracy of the type.

Primitive does not mean $M_\Phi=T_K$. Primitivity is an isogeny-simplicity condition; nondegeneracy is a stronger rank statement about the Galois orbit. An induced type forces visible row repetitions and a smaller torus, but the exact rank depends on the inducing data. For an imaginary quadratic field, the two conjugate characteristic vectors span $\mathbf Q^2$, so $M_\Phi=T_K$. In higher degree the rank can be strictly smaller than $2g$.

### 12.3 The reciprocity interface

Given $(K,\Phi)$, polarized ideal data $(\mathfrak a,\xi)$, and a finite idele $s\in\mathbf A_{E,f}^\times$, structural CM theory produces

$$
r_\Phi(s)\in\mathbf A_{K,f}^\times, \tag{12.5}
$$

a new lattice class through the inverse ideal action, and a polarization multiplier controlled by

$$
r_\Phi(s)\overline{r_\Phi(s)}=N_{E/\mathbf Q}(s). \tag{12.6}
$$

Global class field theory, with arithmetic Frobenius normalization, also associates to the idele class of $s$ an abelianized Galois element

$$
\operatorname{Art}_E(s)\in G_E^{\mathrm{ab}}. \tag{12.7}
$$

The missing assertion is a canonical comparison between the Galois conjugate of a CM abelian variety by (12.7) and the ideal transform built from (12.5), including the correct inverse dictated by the Artin and homology conventions. Nothing in the construction of either side proves that comparison. Establishing it is the main reciprocity law of complex multiplication.

Accordingly, this book does not deduce canonical models over abelian extensions of $E$, identify Galois orbits with reflex ideal-class orbits, compute good-prime Frobenius through reflex ideals, descend every endomorphism or level structure to $E$, or infer good reduction solely from the CM type. Each assertion needs its own reciprocity, descent, and reduction hypotheses.

There is nevertheless a useful diagram of intended arrows:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\mathbf A_{E,f}^\times/E^\times
&\xrightarrow{\operatorname{Art}_E}&G_E^{\mathrm{ab}}\\
\downarrow r_\Phi&&\downarrow\text{Galois action}\\
\mathbf A_{K,f}^\times/K^\times
&\xrightarrow{\text{ideal action}}&
\{\text{CM classes of type }\Phi\}.
\end{array} \tag{12.8}
$$

The left and bottom arrows have been constructed here. Commutativity, the placement of any inverse, canonical descent, and finite-place consequences belong to the reciprocity theorem. The diagram clarifies the goal without using its unproved commutativity as an argument.

### 12.4 Conclusion

A CM field is a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real field, equipped with its positive conjugation. A CM type chooses one embedding from every conjugate pair and turns the rank-one $K$-module $K$ into a weight $-1$ Hodge structure. Full lattices give complex tori, and positive imaginary trace forms make every such torus projective. For an arbitrary lattice, integrality is exactly $\xi\bar{\mathfrak a}\subseteq\mathfrak a^t$. For a proper invertible ideal of a CM order $\mathcal O$, integrality and principality become

$$
\xi\bar{\mathfrak a}\mathfrak a
\subseteq\mathcal O^t,
\qquad
\xi\mathfrak a\bar{\mathfrak a}
=\mathcal O^t
\text{ in the principal case}.
$$

For the maximal order, $\mathcal O^t=\mathfrak D_K^{-1}$. These formulas classify polarized CM abelian varieties over $\mathbf C$, identify Rosati with conjugation, and explain how invertible ideals act through $\mathfrak b^{-1}\mathfrak a$. Primitive types are exactly those yielding simple isogeny factors. Orders and conductor primes record where the class-group action and prime-to-level descriptions require qualification.

The type stabilizer defines the reflex field $E$. The Hodge cocharacter defines, without choices, the reflex norm

$$
r_\Phi:\operatorname{Res}_{E/\mathbf Q}\mathbf G_m
\longrightarrow\operatorname{Res}_{K/\mathbf Q}\mathbf G_m.
$$

Its direction distinguishes it from the type norm. Its inverse-set formula explains the reflex type, its image is the Mumford--Tate torus, and its weight identity

$$
r_\Phi(b)\overline{r_\Phi(b)}=N_{E/\mathbf Q}(b)
$$

controls polarization similitudes on elements, ideals, and finite ideles. These constructions provide the CM input needed by the PEL models: determinant polynomials over the reflex field, Rosati-compatible order actions, polarized ideal transforms, and prime-to-bad-level control.

What remains is deliberately sharp. Structural CM theory has built both the reflex ideal action and the abelianized Galois action, but it has not identified them. The main theorem of complex multiplication must prove that reciprocity comparison and then derive canonical descent, reduction, and Frobenius consequences. The present volume therefore supplies a complete geometric and algebraic foundation without borrowing the theorem it is meant to support.
